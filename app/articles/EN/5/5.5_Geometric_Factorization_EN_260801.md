## §1 Four-Factor Structure

### 1.1 Decomposition Theorem

**Theorem 5.5.1.01 (Geometric Factorization Theorem).** Any observable $O$ of the information field can be decomposed into the product of four geometric factors:

$$O = f_1(\Lambda) \cdot f_2(\Delta\Theta) \cdot f_3(k_0) \cdot f_4(\gamma_{\text{Berry}}),$$

where:
- $f_1(\Lambda)$: Matter Sector factor, depends on $\Lambda = 3$
- $f_2(\Delta\Theta)$: Causal Sector factor, depends on $\Delta\Theta = 5$
- $f_3(k_0)$: Information Sector factor, depends on $k_0 = 2$
- $f_4(\gamma_{\text{Berry}})$: Closing Step factor, depends on Berry phase $= 2\pi$

**Proof.** The coding orbit consists of four segments: the ℳ segment (Cl(1)→Cl(3)), the 𝒞 segment (Cl(3)→Cl(5)), the ℐ segment (Cl(5)→Cl(7)), and the closing step (Cl(7)→Cl(8)→Cl(1)). Each segment corresponds to an algebraic structure jump point, producing an independent geometric factor.

An observable $O$ is a functional of the coding orbit — $O = F[\sigma^*, T_{\text{ours}}]$. Since the coding orbit is partitioned into four segments, the functional $F$ can be decomposed into the product of four segments:

$$F = F_{\mathcal{M}} \times F_{\mathcal{C}} \times F_{\mathcal{I}} \times F_{\text{close}}.$$

The functional of each segment depends only on the structure constants of that segment: $F_{\mathcal{M}} = f_1(\Lambda)$, $F_{\mathcal{C}} = f_2(\Delta\Theta)$, $F_{\mathcal{I}} = f_3(k_0)$, $F_{\text{close}} = f_4(\gamma_{\text{Berry}})$. Hence $O = f_1 \cdot f_2 \cdot f_3 \cdot f_4$. ∎

### 1.2 Independence of Factors

The four factors correspond to the four structure jump points of the coding orbit and are mutually independent — changing one does not affect the others. This is because the four jump points occupy inescapable positions in the Bott periodic table:

| Jump Point | Algebraic Transition | Structure Constant | Bott Period |
|:---:|:---|:---:|:---|
| ℳ segment | Cl(1)→Cl(3) | Λ = 3 | Real → Quaternionic |
| 𝒞 segment | Cl(3)→Cl(5) | ΔΘ = 5 | Quaternionic → Complex |
| ℐ segment | Cl(5)→Cl(7) | k₀ = 2 | Complex → Real (Terminal Duality) |
| Closing Step | Cl(7)→Cl(8)→Cl(1) | γ = 2π | Bott Period Closure |

The four jump points are non-commutative in the Bott periodic table — each step must be completed in order. Thus the four factors are independent — changing one structure constant does not affect the other factors.

### 1.6 Detailed Derivation

**Detailed derivation of Theorem 5.5.1.01.** The derivation of the Geometric Factorization Theorem proceeds in the following five steps:

**Step 1: Four-segment partition of the coding orbit.** The coding orbit $\Gamma$ departs from Cl(1), passes through four key jump points of the Bott period, and finally returns to Cl(1). According to the Coding Orbit Theorem of Volume 2 (Theorem 2.2.1.01), the orbit is partitioned by the three sector boundaries into four segments:
$$\Gamma = \Gamma_{\mathcal{M}} \cup \Gamma_{\mathcal{C}} \cup \Gamma_{\mathcal{I}} \cup \Gamma_{\text{close}},$$
where $\Gamma_{\mathcal{M}}$ corresponds to Cl(1)→Cl(3) (Matter Sector), $\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}$ corresponds to Cl(3)→Cl(5) (Causal Sector), $\Gamma_{\mathcal{I}}$ corresponds to Cl(5)→Cl(7) (Information Sector), and $\Gamma_{\text{close}}$ corresponds to Cl(7)→Cl(8)→Cl(1) (closing step). The legitimacy of the four-segment partition comes from the three inescapable jump points in the Bott periodic table — Cl(1)→Cl(3) (Real → Quaternionic), Cl(3)→Cl(5) (Quaternionic → Complex), Cl(5)→Cl(7) (Complex → Real), together with the closing step Cl(7)→Cl(8)→Cl(1) (closure of Bott period 8).

**Step 2: Product decomposition of the functional.** An observable $O$ is a functional of the coding orbit — $O = F[\sigma^*, T_{\text{ours}}]$. Since $\sigma^*$ is the Birkhoff fixed point (Theorem 3.3.1.01), it defines a unique parametrization on the coding orbit. The functional $F$ integrates along the orbit and can be decomposed into the product of four segment integrals:
$$F[\sigma^*, T_{\text{ours}}] = \int_{\Gamma} \mathcal{L}[\sigma, T] \, ds = \prod_{k=1}^{4} \int_{\Gamma_k} \mathcal{L}_k[\sigma, T] \, ds,$$
where $\mathcal{L}_k$ is the Lagrangian density of the $k$-th segment. The legitimacy of the product decomposition comes from the independence of the Bott period jumps between the four segments — each jump occupies an inescapable position in the Bott periodic table, producing non-commutative algebraic structure changes. Hence the product decomposition of the functional is exact, not approximate.

**Step 3: Factor forms determined by the Born Rule.** The value of each segment integral $\int_{\Gamma_k} \mathcal{L}_k \, ds$ is determined by the structure constant of that segment and the Born Rule $\Delta\lambda \propto \sqrt{\sigma}$ (Theorem 3.2.2.01). Specifically:
- $\Gamma_{\mathcal{M}}$ segment: $\sigma_{\mathcal{M}} \propto \Lambda = 3$, the Born Rule gives $\int_{\Gamma_{\mathcal{M}}} \mathcal{L} \, ds = \sqrt{\Lambda} = \sqrt{3}$.
- $\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}$ segment: $\sigma_{\mathcal{C}} \propto \Delta\Theta = 5$, but $\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}$ is the continuation of $\Gamma_{\mathcal{M}}$, so the relative factor is $\sqrt{\Delta\Theta/\Lambda} = \sqrt{5/3}$.
- $\Gamma_{\mathcal{I}}$ segment: $\sigma_{\mathcal{I}} \propto k_0 = 2$, Terminal Duality gives $\int_{\Gamma_{\mathcal{I}}} \mathcal{L} \, ds = \sqrt{k_0} = \sqrt{2}$.
- $\Gamma_{\text{close}}$ segment: Berry phase $\gamma = 2\pi$ is a global topological quantity, normalization gives $\int_{\Gamma_{\text{close}}} \mathcal{L} \, ds = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}$.

**Step 4: Verification of factor independence.** The four factors correspond to four distinct jump points in the Bott periodic table. Changing $\Lambda$ would require changing the number of jump steps from Cl(1)→Cl(3), but in Bott periodicity Cl(1)→Cl(3) is fixed (Real → Quaternionic transition, 2 steps), hence $\Lambda = 3$ is a fixed value. Similarly, $\Delta\Theta = 5$ (Cl(3)→Cl(5), 2 steps), $k_0 = 2$ (Cl(5)→Cl(7) Terminal Duality), and $\gamma = 2\pi$ (Bott period closure) are all fixed values. The four factors are independent and fixed — this is the core meaning of "geometric factors": uniquely determined by the algebraic-topological structure of the coding orbit, unaffected by landscape position.

**Step 5: Product simplification reveals pairing symmetry.** The simplified computation of the four-factor product reveals an intrinsic symmetry of the algebraic structure:
$$f_1 f_2 f_3 f_4 = \sqrt{3} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{5}{3}} \cdot \sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} = \frac{\sqrt{3} \cdot \sqrt{5} \cdot \sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3} \cdot \sqrt{2\pi}} = \sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}.$$
After simplification, only $\Delta\Theta = 5$ and $\pi$ remain — the Matter Sector factor $\sqrt{3}$ and the Information Sector factor $\sqrt{2}$ cancel each other in the product. This means $\Lambda = 3$ and $k_0 = 2$ are "paired" at the product level — their effects cancel each other in the closure of the coding orbit, leaving only $\Delta\Theta = 5$ as the "net geometric factor." This pairing comes from the symmetry between Cl(1)→Cl(3) and Cl(5)→Cl(7) in Bott periodicity — both are jumps of dimension increase by 2, but in opposite directions.

### 1.8 Comparative Discussion

**Geometric Factorization vs Renormalization Group Factorization.** Geometric factorization has a structural correspondence with renormalization group (RG) factorization in quantum field theory. In RG factorization, an observable $O$ is decomposed into the product of a Wilson coefficient $C(\mu)$ and an operator matrix element $\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle(\mu)$: $O = C(\mu) \times \langle\mathcal{O}\rangle(\mu)$, where $\mu$ is the renormalization scale. Key differences between the two factorizations:

| Feature | Geometric Factorization (CSG) | RG Factorization (QFT) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Number of factors | Fixed at 4 | Not fixed (depends on operator mixing) |
| Scale dependence | None (structure constants fixed) | Yes (depends on μ) |
| Factor independence | Strictly independent (Bott period rigidity) | Depends on renormalization scheme |
| Universality | Landscape-universal (all worlds) | Model-dependent |
| Parameter origin | Coding orbit topology | Perturbation theory + experimental fitting |

The most important difference: the number of factors in RG factorization depends on the mixing pattern of operators in the effective field theory — different processes may involve different numbers of factors. The number of factors in geometric factorization is fixed at 4 — the four-segment partition of the coding orbit is unique and inescapable. The factorization of any observable involves exactly 4 factors, no more and no less. This is a strong constraint imposed by the topological rigidity of the coding orbit on physics.

**Geometric Factorization vs mass-energy equivalence $E = mc^2$.** Both are paradigms of "factorization." $E = mc^2$ decomposes energy into the product of mass $m$ and the speed of light squared $c^2$ — $m$ is a dynamical quantity (intrinsic to the object), $c$ is a universal constant (spacetime structure). Similarly, geometric factorization decomposes an observable into the product of a dynamical part $S(\sigma^*)$ and the geometric factors $f_1 f_2 f_3 f_4$ — $S(\sigma^*)$ is a dynamical quantity (landscape position), $f_1 f_2 f_3 f_4$ are universal constants (coding orbit topology). The difference: $c$ is a single universal constant, while geometric factorization involves 4 universal factors — corresponding to the four-segment structure of the coding orbit.

### 2.2 Factorization of Mass Ratios

In the factorization of particle mass ratios $m_i/m_j$, $f_4$ (the Berry phase factor) does not appear in the ratio (since it is the same for all particles). Hence mass ratios involve only three factors:

$$\frac{m_i}{m_j} = \frac{f_1^{(i)} f_2^{(i)} f_3^{(i)}}{f_1^{(j)} f_2^{(j)} f_3^{(j)}}.$$

If two particles lie in the same sector (e.g., both in the ℳ sector), then $f_1, f_2, f_3$ are identical, and the mass ratio reduces to the coding weight ratio:

$$\frac{m_i}{m_j} = \frac{\sqrt{\sigma_i}}{\sqrt{\sigma_j}} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_j}}.$$

This is a direct application of the Born Rule — the mass ratio is the square root of the coding weight ratio.

### 2.7 Comparative Discussion

### 5.7 Comparative Discussion

**Completeness of factorization vs "incompleteness" of the Standard Model.** In the Standard Model, different observables are parametrized in different ways: masses are parametrized by Yukawa couplings, coupling constants by gauge couplings, mixing angles by CKM/PMNS matrices, and the Higgs potential by $\mu^2$ and $\lambda$. The factorization of Conjugate Spectral Geometry provides a unified parametrization structure: all observables are expressed in terms of the four geometric factors and the dynamical part $S(\sigma^*)$. This is a "topological symmetry → structural decomposition" correspondence — analogous to Noether's Theorem, but operating at the structural level rather than the conserved quantity level.

The factorization of Conjugate Spectral Geometry provides a unified decomposition structure: all processes involve the same four geometric factors, differing only in the powers $n_k$ of each factor. This is analogous to Fourier analysis, where all signals are decomposed into the same sine/cosine basis functions, differing only in coefficients — geometric factors are the "Fourier basis" of physics.
