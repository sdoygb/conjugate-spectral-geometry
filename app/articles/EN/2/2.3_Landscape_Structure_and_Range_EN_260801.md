## §1 Concept of the Landscape

### 1.1 What Is the Landscape

**Definition 12 (Landscape).** The landscape $\mathcal{L}$ is the set of all possible $S$ values:
$$\mathcal{L} = \{S(\sigma^*(T)) : T \in \mathcal{B}_2\},$$
where $\mathcal{B}_2$ is the 2-dimensional Birkhoff polytope (the parameter space of $T$), $\sigma^*(T)$ is the fixed point for a given $T$, and $S(\sigma^*)$ is the value of the algebraic action at the fixed point.

### 1.2 Dimensionality of the Landscape

$T$ varies on a 2-dimensional polytope ($\theta_I + \theta_\tau + \theta_{\sigma_2} = 1$, 2 degrees of freedom). $S$ is a function of $T$, so the range of $S$ is a 1-dimensional projection of a 2-dimensional parameter space — in general, the contour lines of $S$ are 1-dimensional curves.

### 1.3 Key Insight

$\alpha^{-1} \approx 137$ is not uniquely determined — it corresponds to a 1-dimensional contour line in the landscape (rather than an isolated point). This means infinitely many $T$ values yield $S \approx 137$.

Uniform sampling of $T$ parameters on the 2-dimensional Birkhoff polytope:

$$\{(\theta_I, \theta_\tau, \theta_{\sigma_2}) : \theta_I + \theta_\tau + \theta_{\sigma_2} = 1, \theta_k \geq 0, \theta_k \in \{0, 0.01, 0.02, \dots, 1\}\}.$$

For each $T$, compute the fixed point $\sigma^*(T)$ and $S(\sigma^*(T))$.

### 2.3 Semispinor Decomposition of $S_{\min}$

**Lemma 2.3.2.02 (Semispinor Decomposition of $S_{\min}$).**
$$S_{\min} = \Lambda \times \dim_{\mathbb{R}}(S^{\pm}) = 3 \times 8 = 24,$$
where $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(S^{\pm}) = 8$ is the dimension of the semispinor representation of $\mathrm{Spin}(8)$.

**Proof.** Given in two steps.

**(i) Algebraic computation.** At the symmetric ground state $\sigma = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$, the geometric mean
$$p = \sqrt{\sigma_1 \sigma_2 \sigma_3} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{27}} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}.$$
The positive root of the algebraic normalization equation $2pC^3 + C^2 = 1$ is $C = \sqrt{3}/2$. Verification:
$$2 \cdot \frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}} \cdot \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^3 + \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{2}{3\sqrt{3}} \cdot \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{8} + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{4} = 1. \quad \checkmark$$

The algebraic action at the symmetric ground state is
$$S(\sigma) = \frac{\sum_i 1/\sigma_i + \sum_{i<j} 1/\sqrt{\sigma_i \sigma_j}}{C^2} = \frac{9+9}{3/4} = \frac{18}{3/4} = 24.$$

Here $\sum_i 1/\sigma_i = 3 \times 3 = 9$ (three terms, each equal to $3$), $\sum_{i<j} 1/\sqrt{\sigma_i \sigma_j} = 3 \times 3 = 9$ (three pairs, each equal to $3$).

**(ii) Semispinor interpretation.** The three sectors $\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{I}, \mathcal{M}$ correspond respectively to the three $8$-dimensional representations $8_v, 8_s, 8_c$ of $\mathrm{Spin}(8)$ triality:

- $\mathcal{C}$ (Causal Sector) $\leftrightarrow 8_v$ (vector representation)
- $\mathcal{I}$ (Information Sector) $\leftrightarrow 8_s$ (spinor representation)
- $\mathcal{M}$ (Matter Sector) $\leftrightarrow 8_c$ (conjugate spinor representation)

Each sector contributes $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(S^{\pm}) = 8$ degrees of freedom, and the three sectors together contribute $3 \times 8 = 24$. Hence
$$S_{\min} = \Lambda \times \dim_{\mathbb{R}}(S^{\pm}) = 3 \times 8 = 24. \qquad \blacksquare$$

This decomposition shows that $S_{\min} = 24$ is not a numerical coincidence but an exact projection of $\mathrm{Spin}(8)$ triality symmetry onto the algebraic action — the symmetric ground state activates all three $8$-dimensional representations, each contributing $8$ coding degrees of freedom.

### 2.4 Derivation of $S_{\max}$

$S_{\max}$ corresponds to the extreme asymmetric configuration — one sector dominates, the other two approach zero. Consider the limit $\sigma \to (1, 0, 0)$:

- Geometric mean $p = \sqrt{\sigma_1 \sigma_2 \sigma_3} \to 0$
- The algebraic normalization equation degenerates to $C^2 = 1$, i.e., $C \to 1$
- The action $S(\sigma) = \left(\sum_i 1/\sigma_i + \sum_{i<j} 1/\sqrt{\sigma_i \sigma_j}\right)/C^2 \to \sum_i 1/\sigma_i \to \infty$

In theory $S$ can diverge to infinity. But due to the boundary constraints of the Birkhoff polytope, $\sigma$ never completely degenerates to $(1, 0, 0)$ — the vertices of the polytope correspond to pure permutation matrices, not degenerate probability distributions. At the grid resolution of the numerical scan (step size $0.01$), the most extreme accessible configuration yields
$$S_{\max} \approx 968.$$

The exact value of $S_{\max}$ depends on the grid resolution — as the grid is refined, $S_{\max}$ will increase further. But physically, the divergence of $S_{\max}$ means that in extremely asymmetric worlds the electromagnetic coupling strength approaches zero ($\alpha \to 0$), and such worlds cannot host observers (see the observer spectral conditions in 2.4).

### 2.5 Physical Meaning of the $C$ Parameter

The parameter $C$ is the projection incompleteness parameter, satisfying the self-consistent relation
$$C^2 = \sum_i w_i^2 = \sum_i \sigma_i \cdot C^2,$$
where $w_i = \sqrt{\sigma_i} \cdot C$ are the coding weights. $C$ measures the degree of incompleteness of the coding projection — the closer $C$ is to $1$, the more complete the projection, but the larger the action $S$ (the more extreme).

## §3 Physical Implications

### 3.1 Why 137?

$S \approx 137$ is not a unique output of the theory — it is the coordinate reading of our region in the landscape. The fact that it takes this particular value is a **regional property**, analogous to the fact that Earth's surface temperature is approximately 288 K — not derivable from first principles, but a consequence of the specific conditions of this region.

### 3.2 Multi-World Structure of the Landscape

The landscape describes a multi-world structure: different $T$ values correspond to different physical worlds, each with its own $\alpha^{-1}$ value. All such worlds are mathematically possible realizations of the coding framework.

### 3.3 Which Worlds Can Host Observers?

Not all worlds in the landscape can host observers. The observer spectral conditions (2.4 §2) restrict the viable range to $100 < S < 200$, approximately 10.6% of the total landscape range.

## §7 Consummate Reproduction, Discrete T Values, and the Parameter Occupation Theorem

### 7.1 Statement of the Problem

The landscape $S \in [24, 968]$ is obtained by treating $T$ as a continuous variable. But in the Consummate Reproduction framework, $T$ is **discrete**.

### 7.2 Consummate Reproduction Theorem

**Theorem 2.3.7.01 (Consummate Reproduction Theorem).** When a region's structure constants $\{2,3,5\}$ satisfy the interlock property (Proposition 0.5.2.01) and the Completeness Criterion (Theorem 0.4.1.01), the region's algebraic structure undergoes consummate reproduction — the Berry phase loop $\delta^8$ closes with $\gamma_{\text{Berry}} = 2\pi$, and the structure reproduces as a Clifford configuration.

### 7.3 Generation of T Values — Determinacy of Our Region and Global Openness

Consummate reproduction, via $D_4$ triality $S_3$ permutations, produces **at most 6 discrete $T$ values** (the 6 elements of $S_3$). Our region adopts one of them — $T_{\text{ours}}$. Which specific $T$ value our region adopts is determined by the shared information layer, not by our region's internal algebra.

### 7.4 Parameter Occupation Theorem

**Theorem 2.3.7.02 (Parameter Occupation Theorem).** Each discrete $T$ value can be occupied by at most one Clifford region (analogous to the Pauli exclusion principle). The 6 possible $T$ values correspond to 6 possible Clifford regions, each with its own set of physical constants.

### 7.5 Analogy with the Pauli Exclusion Principle

Just as two fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state, two Clifford regions cannot occupy the same $T$ value. This is not a physical law imposed from outside — it is a consequence of the information-theoretic structure of the shared information layer: if two regions shared the same $T$, their physical constants would be identical, making them indistinguishable at the information level, violating the distinguishability requirement of the coding framework.

### 7.6 Impact on Landscape Structure

The continuous landscape $S \in [24, 968]$ is the mathematical possibility space. The physically realized landscape consists of discrete points — one for each occupied $T$ value.
