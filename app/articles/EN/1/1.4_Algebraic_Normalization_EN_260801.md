## Abstract
This paper supplements four key items: (1) a complete algebraic proof of the sector weight normalization $\sum\sigma_i = 1$ — this normalization is not a postulate, but a theorem rigorously derived from the algebraic structure of $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$; (2) a complete derivation of the cubic equation $2pC^3 + C^2 = 1$, based on trigonometric identities under $\theta_1 + \theta_2 + \theta_3 = \pi/2$ and the algebraic substitution of the Born Rule; (3) the discriminant, real root structure, radical (trigonometric) solution, and numerical verification of the cubic equation; (4) a rigorous proof of $C^2<1$ and an analysis of Parseval incompatibility, clarifying why normalization must choose $\sum\theta=\pi/2$ (the algebraic path) rather than the Parseval condition.

## §1 Introduction and Problem

$$\theta_M + \theta_C + \theta_I = \frac{\pi}{2}.$$

This is a **transcendental constraint** — it involves the $\arcsin$ function and cannot be solved exactly by algebraic (polynomial) methods. The existence of a transcendental constraint means that there is a step in the derivation chain that cannot be algebraized.

### 1.1 Problem

The transcendental constraint leads to:
1. The appearance of transcendental functions in the derivation chain, preventing verification by purely algebraic methods
2. Numerical solutions introducing floating-point errors, affecting exactness
3. Theoretical structural non-self-consistency — transcendental constraints should not appear in an algebraic framework

## §2 Algebraic Proof of Sector Weight Normalization $\sum\sigma_i = 1$
### 2.1 $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$ Sector Decomposition and Projection Intensity

Let $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$ be the Clifford algebra of three-dimensional Euclidean space, isomorphic to $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ (the ring of $2 \times 2$ complex matrices). Its three generators $e_1, e_2, e_3$ satisfy the Clifford relations:
$$e_i e_j + e_j e_i = 2\delta_{ij}, \quad i,j = 1,2,3.$$

**Left-multiplication representation.** Take the standard representation of $\mathrm{Cl}(3) \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ on $\mathbb{C}^2 \cong \mathbb{R}^4$. The left-multiplication action of the three generators yields real linear maps $L_1, L_2, L_3$ on $\mathbb{R}^4$ (i.e., the realization of the Pauli matrices):
$$L_i : \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}^4, \quad L_i^2 = \mathrm{id}, \quad L_i L_j + L_j L_i = 2\delta_{ij}\,\mathrm{id}.$$

These three operators constitute an irreducible representation of $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$, encoding the algebraic structure of the three sectors ($M, C, I$).

**Rank-2 orthogonal projection.** The holographic projection in the coding space is described by a rank-2 orthogonal projection $P: \mathbb{R}^4 \to V$ ($\dim V = 2$). The projection $P$ selects a two-dimensional plane $V$ in $\mathbb{R}^4$, representing the coding subspace jointly covered by the three sectors.

### 2.2 Projection Intensity Identity

**Lemma 1.4.2.01 (Projection Intensity).** For any rank-2 orthogonal projection $P$, there exist real numbers $c_1, c_2, c_3$ such that
$$P L_i P = c_i \cdot J, \quad i = 1,2,3,$$
where $J$ is the unique complex structure ($J^2 = -\mathrm{id}_V$) on the plane $V$ induced by the volume element $\omega = e_1 e_2 e_3$ of $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$.

**Proof.** The plane $V$, as a two-dimensional oriented subspace of $\mathbb{R}^4$, has an almost complex structure $J$ induced by the volume element of $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$. The compression $PL_iP$ of the operator $L_i$ onto $V$ is a linear operator on $V$; since $\dim V = 2$, the space of anti-self-adjoint operators on $V$ is one-dimensional, spanned by $J$. Hence $PL_iP$ must be a scalar multiple of $J$. Let $PL_iP = c_i J$; the scalars $c_i$ are precisely the **projection intensities**. ∎

**Coding weights and projection intensity.** The coding weight $\sigma_i$ is defined as the square of the projection intensity:
$$\sigma_i = c_i^2, \quad i = M, C, I.$$
This definition ensures $\sigma_i \geq 0$, and $\sigma_i$ measures the coding strength of sector $i$ under the projection $P$.

### 2.3 Sector Weight Normalization (Algebraic Re-proof of Theorem 1.3.2.02)

**Theorem 1.3.2.02 (Sector Weight Normalization).** Under the $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$ sector decomposition, the coding weights satisfy:
$$\sigma_M + \sigma_C + \sigma_I = 1.$$

> This section provides an algebraic re-proof of this theorem based on the quaternion cross product identity. The original proof is in 1.3 §2.4.

**Proof (based on the Projection Intensity Identity and the quaternion cross product identity).**

**Step 1.** Let $u, v$ be an orthonormal basis of the plane $V$ ($\|u\| = \|v\| = 1$, $\langle u, v\rangle = 0$). The projection $P$ is given by $Px = \langle x, u\rangle u + \langle x, v\rangle v$.

**Step 2.** Compute the projection intensities $c_i$. From $PL_iP = c_i J$, taking matrix elements with respect to the basis vectors yields:
$$c_i = \langle L_i u, v\rangle.$$

That is, $c_i$ is precisely the component of $L_i u$ in the $v$ direction — the inner product of $L_i u$ projected onto $v$.

**Step 3.** Compute $\sum c_i^2$. From $c_i = \langle L_i u, v\rangle$:
$$\sum_{i=1}^{3} c_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{3} \langle L_i u, v\rangle^2.$$

**Step 4 (Quaternion cross product identity).** Identify $\mathbb{R}^4 \cong \mathbb{H}$ (quaternions). The left-multiplication operators $L_1, L_2, L_3$ correspond to left-multiplication by the quaternion units $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$. For quaternions $u, v$, there is the identity:
$$\sum_{i=1}^{3} \langle \mathbf{e}_i \, u, v\rangle^2 = \|u\|^2 \|v\|^2 - \langle u, v\rangle^2,$$
where $\mathbf{e}_i \in \{\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}\}$. This is the quaternionic version of the **Lagrange identity** — the left-hand side is the sum of squares of the cross product components, and the right-hand side is the Gram determinant.

**Step 5.** For the orthonormal basis $u, v$ ($\|u\|=\|v\|=1$, $\langle u,v\rangle=0$):
$$\sum_{i=1}^{3} c_i^2 = \|u\|^2\|v\|^2 - \langle u,v\rangle^2 = 1 - 0 = 1.$$

**Step 6.** From $\sigma_i = c_i^2$:
$$\sigma_M + \sigma_C + \sigma_I = \sum_{i=1}^{3} c_i^2 = 1. \qquad \blacksquare$$

### 2.4 Algebraic Roots of the Normalization — Non-Postulate Nature

The key point of the above proof is: $\sum\sigma_i = 1$ is **not** introduced as a postulate assumed for completeness, but is a theorem derived from the following three purely algebraic facts:

1. **Representation structure of $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$**: $L_i$ satisfy the Clifford relations (algebraic axiom)
2. **Projection-complex structure correspondence**: $PL_iP = c_i J$ (linear algebra lemma)
3. **Quaternionic Lagrange identity**: $\sum c_i^2 = \|u\|^2\|v\|^2 - \langle u,v\rangle^2$ (algebraic identity)

None of these three premises is an assumption about coding weights — they are all intrinsic properties of the algebraic structure of $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$. Hence $\sum\sigma_i = 1$ is a **theorem** of the $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$ algebra, whose status is equivalent to an algebraic fact such as "the representation of $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$ is four-dimensional."

This conclusion directly affects the subsequent derivation of the cubic equation: the step using $\sum\sigma_i = 1$ (see §3.3) has a rigorous algebraic foundation, not a circular argument.

## §3 Algebraic Normalization Condition

### 3.1 From Angles to Sines

From the Born Rule (Theorem 1.3.4.01–20), the coding angles are related to the coding weights via the sine function:
$$\sin\theta_i = \frac{\sqrt{\sigma_i}}{S}, \quad S = \sum_j \sqrt{\sigma_j}.$$

The normalization condition should directly constrain $\sin\theta_i$, not $\theta_i$ itself — this avoids the appearance of $\arcsin$.

### 3.2 Incompleteness Parameter

**Definition 20 (Incompleteness Parameter).** Let
$$C^2 = \sin^2\theta_M\sin^2\theta_C\sin^2\theta_I.$$

$C^2$ measures the "incompleteness" of the holographic projection — the sum of the intensities of the three projection directions.

- $C^2 = 1$: complete projection (three directions cover all information)
- $C^2 < 1$: incomplete projection (information loss exists)

From $\sin\theta_i = \sqrt{\sigma_i}/S$ and $\sum\sigma_i = 1$ (theorem, §2):
$$C^2 = \sum_i \sin^2\theta_i = \frac{\sum_i \sigma_i}{S^2} = \frac{1}{S^2}, \quad \Rightarrow \quad S = \frac{1}{C}, \quad \sin\theta_i = \sqrt{\sigma_i}\cdot C.$$

### 3.3 Complete Derivation of the Cubic Equation

**Theorem 1.4.3.01 (Algebraic Normalization).** The normalization constant $C$ satisfies the cubic equation:
$$2p\,C^3 + C^2 = 1, \qquad p = \sqrt{\sigma_M \sigma_C \sigma_I}.$$

**Proof.**

#### Key Trigonometric Identity

**Lemma 1.4.3.02 (Three-Sine Identity).** When $\theta_1 + \theta_2 + \theta_3 = \pi/2$, the following identity holds:
$$\sin^2\theta_1 + \sin^2\theta_2 + \sin^2\theta_3 + 2\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2\sin\theta_3 = 1.$$

**Proof.** From $\theta_3 = \pi/2 - \theta_1 - \theta_2$, we have $\sin\theta_3 = \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$. Expanding:
$$\sin^2\theta_3 = \cos^2(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = 1 - \sin^2(\theta_1 + \theta_2).$$

Now expand $\sin^2(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$. From $\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = \sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2$:
$$\sin^2(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = \sin^2\theta_1\cos^2\theta_2 + \cos^2\theta_1\sin^2\theta_2 + 2\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2.$$

Substituting back into $\sin^2\theta_3$:
$$\sin^2\theta_3 = 1 - \sin^2\theta_1\cos^2\theta_2 - \cos^2\theta_1\sin^2\theta_2 - 2\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2\cos\theta_2.$$

The sum of the three $\sin^2$ terms is:
$$\sin^2\theta_1 + \sin^2\theta_2 + \sin^2\theta_3 = \sin^2\theta_1(1-\cos^2\theta_2) + \sin^2\theta_2(1-\cos^2\theta_1) + 1 - 2\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2\cos\theta_2.$$

Using $1-\cos^2 = \sin^2$ to simplify:
$$= \sin^2\theta_1\sin^2\theta_2 + \sin^2\theta_2\sin^2\theta_1 + 1 - 2\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2\cos\theta_2$$
$$= 1 + 2\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2\bigl(\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2 - \cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2\bigr).$$

Note that $\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2 - \cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 = -\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = -\sin\theta_3$, hence:
$$\sin^2\theta_1 + \sin^2\theta_2 + \sin^2\theta_3 = 1 - 2\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2\sin\theta_3.$$

Rearranging yields:
$$\sin^2\theta_1 + \sin^2\theta_2 + \sin^2\theta_3 + 2\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2\sin\theta_3 = 1. \qquad\blacksquare$$

#### Algebraic Derivation

Substitute the algebraic form of the Born Rule $\sin\theta_i = \sqrt{\sigma_i}\cdot C$ (where $C = 1/S = 1/\Lambda$, $\Lambda$ being the normalization constant) into the Three-Sine Identity.

**Individual terms.** Each $\sin^2\theta_i$ contributes $\sigma_i C^2$, and the sum of the three is:
$$\sum_i \sin^2\theta_i = \Bigl(\sum_i \sigma_i\Bigr) C^2 = 1 \cdot C^2 = C^2,$$
where the third equality uses the sector weight normalization $\sum\sigma_i = 1$ (§2 theorem).

**Cross term.** The product $\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2\sin\theta_3$ contributes:
$$\sin\theta_M\sin\theta_C\sin\theta_I = \sqrt{\sigma_M\sigma_C\sigma_I}\cdot C^3 = p\cdot C^3,$$
where $p = \sqrt{\sigma_M\sigma_C\sigma_I}$ is an algebraic parameter fully determined by the coding weights.

**Combining.** Substitute the individual terms and the cross term into the identity $\sum\sin^2\theta_i + 2\prod\sin\theta_i = 1$:
$$\underbrace{\Bigl(\sum_i \sigma_i\Bigr)}_{=\,1}\cdot C^2 + 2\underbrace{\sqrt{\sigma_M\sigma_C\sigma_I}}_{=\,p}\cdot C^3 = 1.$$

That is:
$$\boxed{2p\,C^3 + C^2 = 1, \qquad p = \sqrt{\sigma_M\sigma_C\sigma_I}.}$$

The coefficient $2$ comes from the coefficient of the cross term in the trigonometric identity (originating from the binomial symmetry of the expansion of $\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$), and $p$ is fully determined by the coding weights. The entire derivation involves only polynomial operations and algebraic identities, with no transcendental functions appearing. $\blacksquare$

### 3.4 Properties of the Cubic Equation

The cubic equation $f(C) = 2pC^3 + C^2 - 1 = 0$ has the following properties.

**(1) Discriminant and non-negativity.** The discriminant of the cubic $aC^3 + bC^2 + cC + d = 0$ (here $a=2p, b=1, c=0, d=-1$) is:
$$\Delta = 18abcd - 4b^3d + b^2c^2 - 4ac^3 - 27a^2d^2 = 4 - 108p^2.$$

By the AM-GM inequality, for nonnegative $\sigma_i$ satisfying $\sum\sigma_i=1$:
$$\sigma_M\sigma_C\sigma_I \leq \left(\frac{\sigma_M + \sigma_C + \sigma_I}{3}\right)^3 = \frac{1}{27},$$
hence $p = \sqrt{\sigma_M\sigma_C\sigma_I} \leq \dfrac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}$, and:
$$\Delta = 4 - 108p^2 \geq 4 - \frac{108}{27} = 4 - 4 = 0 \geq 0.$$

The discriminant $\Delta \geq 0$ holds universally, guaranteeing that the roots of the cubic are all real.

**(2) Three real roots (casus irreducibilis).** When the $\sigma$ are not fully symmetric, $p < 1/(3\sqrt{3})$, so $\Delta > 0$ and the cubic has three distinct real roots. This is the **irreducible case** (casus irreducibilis) of the cubic — although three real roots exist, they cannot be expressed solely with real radicals and require trigonometric functions (see §3.5).

**(3) Uniqueness of the positive root.** Examine $f(C) = 2pC^3 + C^2 - 1$:
- $f(0) = -1 < 0$,
- $f(1) = 2p > 0$ (when $p>0$),
- $f'(C) = 6pC^2 + 2C = C(6pC + 2) > 0$ for $C > 0$.

Thus $f$ is strictly monotonically increasing on $(0,\infty)$ and has exactly one zero in $(0,1)$. Hence the cubic has exactly one positive root $C$ in $(0,1]$, corresponding to the unique physical solution.

**(4) Symmetric limit.** When $\sigma = (1/3,\, 1/3,\, 1/3)$:
$$p = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{27}} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.19245, \quad \Delta = 0.$$
The cubic has a multiple root. Substituting verifies $C = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$:
$$2\cdot\frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}\cdot\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^3 + \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{2}{3\sqrt{3}}\cdot\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{8} + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{4} = 1. \;\checkmark$$
Hence $C = \sqrt{3}/2 \approx 0.86603$, and the normalization constant $\Lambda = 1/C = 2/\sqrt{3} \approx 1.1547$.

**(5) Extreme limit.** When one sector dominates (e.g., $\sigma_M \to 1$, $\sigma_C, \sigma_I \to 0$), $p \to 0$ and the cubic degenerates to $C^2 = 1$, i.e., $C \to 1$, $\Lambda \to 1$. The holographic projection tends to completeness.

### 3.5 Radical Solution of the Cubic Equation

The cubic $2pC^3 + C^2 - 1 = 0$ can be solved by the **trigonometric method** (suitable for casus irreducibilis).

**Reduction to a depressed cubic.** Dividing by $2p$ ($p > 0$) yields $C^3 + \dfrac{1}{2p}C^2 - \dfrac{1}{2p} = 0$. Substituting $C = t - \dfrac{1}{6p}$ to eliminate the quadratic term gives the depressed cubic:
$$t^3 + Pt + Q = 0, \quad P = -\frac{1}{12p^2}, \quad Q = \frac{1-54p^2}{108p^3}.$$

**Trigonometric solution.** When $\Delta > 0$ (i.e., $4P^3 + 27Q^2 < 0$), the three real roots are:
$$t_k = 2\sqrt{-\frac{P}{3}}\,\cos\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\arccos\!\left(\frac{3Q}{2P}\sqrt{-\frac{3}{P}}\right) - \frac{2\pi k}{3}\right), \quad k=0,1,2.$$

Substituting the expressions for $P$ and $Q$, the components simplify to:
$$2\sqrt{-\frac{P}{3}} = \frac{1}{3p}, \qquad \frac{3Q}{2P}\sqrt{-\frac{3}{P}} = 54p^2 - 1.$$

Hence:
$$\boxed{C_k = -\frac{1}{6p} + \frac{1}{3p}\cos\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\arccos(54p^2 - 1) - \frac{2\pi k}{3}\right), \quad k = 0,1,2.}$$

**Selection of the physical solution.** Among the three roots $C_0, C_1, C_2$, exactly one lies in $(0, 1]$ — the one corresponding to $k=0$ (verifiable by the monotonicity of $f$). This positive root is the normalization constant $C$.

**Verification of the symmetric case.** When $p = 1/(3\sqrt{3})$, $54p^2 - 1 = 1$, $\arccos(1) = 0$, and $k=0$ gives:
$$C_0 = -\frac{1}{6p} + \frac{1}{3p}\cos(0) = \frac{1}{6p} = \frac{1}{6\cdot 1/(3\sqrt{3})} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}. \;\checkmark$$

The entire solution process uses only algebraic operations and trigonometric functions (trigonometric functions here serve as a tool for solving algebraic equations, not as transcendental constraints appearing in the theoretical structure), and does not involve $\arcsin$ or other transcendental constraints.

$C$'s specific value depends on $p$, and $p$ is determined by the coding structure. The value of $p$ is determined in Volume 2 via the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix.

### 3.7 Proof of $C^2 < 1$

**Theorem 1.4.3.03 (Incomplete Projection Theorem).** When $p > 0$ (i.e., all $\sigma_i > 0$), the cubic yields $C < 1$, hence $C^2 < 1$.

**Proof.** Examine $f(C) = 2pC^3 + C^2 - 1$.

- When $p > 0$, $f(1) = 2p\cdot 1^3 + 1^2 - 1 = 2p > 0$.
- $f(0) = -1 < 0$.
- $f$ is strictly monotonically increasing on $(0, \infty)$ ($f'(C) = C(6pC + 2) > 0$ for $C>0$).

From $f(0) < 0 < f(1)$ and the strict monotonicity of $f$, the unique positive zero $C^*$ of $f$ satisfies $0 < C^* < 1$. Hence $C < 1$, and:
$$C^2 < 1.$$

**Physical significance.** $C^2 < 1$ measures the **incompleteness** of the holographic projection — the three projection directions cannot completely cover the coding space. This incompleteness is intrinsic: whenever all three sectors have nonzero weights ($p > 0$), the projection is necessarily incomplete.

**Degenerate case.** Only when $p = 0$ (some $\sigma_i = 0$, i.e., a sector weight is zero) does the cubic degenerate to $C^2 = 1$, $C = 1$, corresponding to a complete projection. The missing sector does not participate in coding, and the projection degenerates to a complete covering of the two-dimensional plane. $\blacksquare$

## §4 Parseval Incompatibility

### 4.1 Conflict of the Two Normalization Constraints

There are two natural candidates for the normalization of the holographic projection:

- **Angle constraint** ($\sum\theta = \pi/2$): the sum of the three coding angles is a right angle, corresponding to the cubic $2pC^3 + C^2=1$.
- **Parseval constraint** ($\sum\sin^2\theta = 1$): the sum of the squares of the three sine components is 1, corresponding to the Parseval identity (completeness).

**Note (Parseval incompatibility).** $\sum\theta = \pi/2$ and $\sum\sin^2\theta = 1$ (the Parseval condition) cannot hold simultaneously unless some angle is zero (degenerate case).

### 4.2 Verification of Symmetric Distribution

Take the symmetric distribution $\sigma = (1/3,\, 1/3,\, 1/3)$ as an example.

**Case A: Angle constraint $\sum\theta = \pi/2$ (cubic).** By symmetry $\theta_M = \theta_C = \theta_I = \pi/6 = 30°$. The cubic gives $C = \sqrt{3}/2$, $C^2 = 3/4$. Verify:
$$\sum\sin^2\theta_i = 3\sin^2 30° = 3\cdot\frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4} = C^2 < 1.$$
The Parseval condition $\sum\sin^2\theta = 1$ is **not satisfied** ($3/4 \neq 1$).

**Case B: Parseval constraint $\sum\sin^2\theta = 1$.** By symmetry $\sin^2\theta_i = 1/3$, $\sin\theta_i = 1/\sqrt{3}$, $\theta_i = \arcsin(1/\sqrt{3}) \approx 35.264°$. Then:
$$\sum\theta_i = 3 \times 35.264° \approx 105.79° \neq 90°.$$
The angle constraint $\sum\theta = \pi/2$ is **not satisfied**.

### 4.3 Conclusion

The two constraints give different angles ($30°$ vs $35.26°$) and different $C^2$ ($3/4$ vs $1$) in the symmetric case, and cannot hold simultaneously. This shows:

1. **A choice must be made for normalization**: $\sum\theta = \pi/2$ (algebraic path, cubic) and $\sum\sin^2\theta = 1$ (Parseval) are mutually exclusive.
2. **This framework chooses the angle constraint**: because $\sum\theta = \pi/2$ is algebraized via the cubic $2pC^3 + C^2=1$, and the entire derivation chain contains no transcendental functions; the Parseval condition, though simple in form, is not self-consistent with the geometric structure of the coding angles.
3. **$C^2 < 1$ is an inevitable consequence**: having chosen the angle constraint, $C^2 = \sum\sin^2\theta < 1$ (Theorem 1.4.3.03), and the intrinsic incompleteness of the holographic projection is naturally encoded by the cubic.

## §6 Prelude to the Algebraic Action

### 6.1 Role of the Normalization

The algebraic normalization $2pC^3 + C^2 = 1$ provides the normalization foundation for the Algebraic Action $S(\sigma)$ (1.5):

- $C$ determines the normalization scale of the coding weights $\sigma_i$
- $C^2 < 1$ measures the information incompleteness — the action $S(\sigma)$ will penalize this incompleteness
- $p$ constrains the structure of the cross term via triality — the cross-term coefficient in the action is determined by $p$

### 6.2 Derivation Chain without Transcendental Functions

The algebraic normalization guarantees that the entire derivation chain contains no transcendental functions:

```
Structure Constant Theorems → Multiplier Sequence (1.1) → Sector Binding (1.2) → Born Rule (1.3)
→ Sector Weight Normalization ∑σ_i=1 (§2, Cl(3) theorem) → Algebraic Normalization 2pC³ + C² = 1 (§3)
→ Algebraic Action S(σ) (1.5) → Birkhoff Mixing Matrix T (2.1) → Fixed Point σ* (2.2) → α⁻¹ (2.2)
```

Every step is an algebraic (polynomial) operation, involving no $\arcsin$, $\exp$, or any transcendental function. The sector weight normalization $\sum\sigma_i=1$, as a $\mathrm{Cl}(3)$ algebraic theorem (§2), further consolidates the algebraic purity of the derivation chain.
