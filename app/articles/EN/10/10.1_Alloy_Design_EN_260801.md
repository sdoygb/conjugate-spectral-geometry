## Abstract

This paper applies the Conjugate Spectral Geometry framework to the field of alloy design. Based on the Many-Body Cross Section Theorem (Theorem 6.5.1.01) and the weight-tuning mechanism of the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix, a geometric prediction theoretical framework for alloy stable phases is established. By mapping alloy composition ratios to Birkhoff weights $(\theta_I, \theta_\tau, \theta_{\sigma_2})$, and using the Jacobian spectral radius minimum of $T^{(N)}$ to determine stable phases, a stability prediction scheme under geometric constraints is provided for superalloy design. This paper further gives the explicit construction of the composition-to-Birkhoff-weight mapping, the proof of the correspondence between Jacobian spectral radius minima and stable phases, and the quantitative prediction of high-temperature stability via the constraint release mechanism.

## §1 Geometric Model

### 1.1 Alloy Composition Birkhoff Weight Mapping

In the Conjugate Spectral Geometry framework, the Sole Axiom δ landscape selects Zero Motion δ as the sole axiom, from which three structural constants emerge: $\Lambda=3$ ($\text{Cl}(3)$ Semisimple Splitting), $k_0=2$ ($\text{Cl}(7)$ Terminal Duality), and $\Delta\Theta=5$ ($\text{Cl}(5)$ Complex Structure Emergence), partitioning three sectors: Material Realm $\mathcal{M}$, Causal Realm $\mathcal{C}$, and Informational Realm $\mathcal{I}$. The Sector-Factor Binding is $\gamma_1 \leftrightarrow 2$, $\gamma_2 \leftrightarrow 3$, $\gamma_3 \leftrightarrow 5$. As a many-body material system, the stable phases of an alloy are determined by the Many-Body Cross Section Theorem.

**Construction of the composition-to-Birkhoff-weight mapping.** Let the component set of an $N$-component alloy be $\{A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_N\}$, with composition ratios $\{c_i\}$ ($\sum c_i = 1$). Each component $A_i$ possesses an intrinsic sector weight vector $(\sigma_{\mathcal{M},i}, \sigma_{\mathcal{C},i}, \sigma_{\mathcal{I},i})$ in the neighborhood of the Observer Localization $\sigma^* \approx (0.777912, 0.210603, 0.011484)$. This intrinsic weight is determined by the electronic structure of the component: the $d$-electron count of transition metals dominates the $\mathcal{M}$-sector weight $\sigma_{\mathcal{M},i}$, the $p$-electrons of main-group elements contribute to the $\mathcal{C}$-sector weight $\sigma_{\mathcal{C},i}$, while the $f$-electrons of rare-earth elements enhance the $\mathcal{I}$-sector weight $\sigma_{\mathcal{I},i}$.

The mapping from composition to Birkhoff weights is a linear superposition:

$$\theta_I = \sum_i c_i \cdot \sigma_{\mathcal{M},i}$$
$$\theta_\tau = \sum_i c_i \cdot \sigma_{\mathcal{C},i}$$
$$\theta_{\sigma_2} = \sum_i c_i \cdot \sigma_{\mathcal{I},i}$$

This mapping satisfies the normalization constraint $\theta_I + \theta_\tau + \theta_{\sigma_2} = 1$, corresponding to a point on the Birkhoff Polytope (weight simplex). The linearity of the mapping guarantees continuous differentiability from composition perturbations $\delta c_i$ to weight perturbations $\delta\theta$, with the Jacobian Matrix:

$$\frac{\partial(\theta_I, \theta_\tau, \theta_{\sigma_2})}{\partial(c_1, \ldots, c_N)} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{\mathcal{M},1} & \cdots & \sigma_{\mathcal{M},N} \\ \sigma_{\mathcal{C},1} & \cdots & \sigma_{\mathcal{C},N} \\ \sigma_{\mathcal{I},1} & \cdots & \sigma_{\mathcal{I},N} \end{bmatrix}$$

The rank of this Jacobian determines the mapping dimension from composition space to weight space. When $\text{rank}=2$ (the typical case for $N \geq 3$), the composition degrees of freedom exceed the weight degrees of freedom, and composition degeneracy exists—multiple composition combinations mapping to the same Birkhoff weight point, corresponding to "iso-weight configurations" of the alloy.

**Theorem 1.01 (Geometric Determination of Alloy Stable Phases).** Let the composition ratios of an $N$-component alloy be $\{c_i\}$ ($\sum c_i = 1$), mapped to Birkhoff weights:

$$\theta_I = \sum_i c_i \cdot \sigma_{\mathcal{M},i}$$
$$\theta_\tau = \sum_i c_i \cdot \sigma_{\mathcal{C},i}$$
$$\theta_{\sigma_2} = \sum_i c_i \cdot \sigma_{\mathcal{I},i}$$

where $(\sigma_{\mathcal{M},i}, \sigma_{\mathcal{C},i}, \sigma_{\mathcal{I},i})$ are the sector weights of the $i$-th component in the neighborhood of the Observer Localization $\sigma^* \approx (0.777912, 0.210603, 0.011484)$. The stable phase of the alloy corresponds to the Jacobian spectral radius $\rho_J$ of the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix $T^{(N)}$ attaining a minimum:

$$\rho_J(T^{(N)}) = \min_{\text{composition space}} |\det(\partial T / \partial \sigma)|$$

This minimum is determined by the fixed-point structure of the Many-Body Cross Section (Theorem 6.5.1.01), i.e., the fixed points of $T^{\otimes N}$ determine the stable configuration of the $N$-body system.

**Proof idea for stable phase = Jacobian spectral radius minimum.** The Many-Body Cross Section Theorem (Theorem 6.5.1.01) shows that the stable configuration of an $N$-body system is determined by the fixed points of $T^{\otimes N}$. The stability of fixed points is determined by the eigenvalues of the Jacobian Matrix $\partial T / \partial \sigma$: when the moduli of all eigenvalues (i.e., the spectral radius $\rho_J$) attain a minimum, the fixed point corresponds to a globally stable configuration. Specifically:

1. When $\rho_J \to 0$, the fixed point is a super-stable node, corresponding to stoichiometrically precise matching phases (e.g., the $\gamma'$ phase of $\text{Ni}_3\text{Al}$)
2. When $\rho_J$ attains a local minimum, it corresponds to metastable phases (e.g., GP zones, $\eta$ phase)
3. When $\rho_J$ attains a global minimum, it corresponds to the thermodynamic equilibrium phase

The contour lines $\rho_J = \text{const}$ of the Jacobian spectral radius give the composition boundaries of stable phases, i.e., the solid solubility limit. This mechanism geometrically explains why the solid solubility limit is not determined solely by the atomic size ratio (a limitation of the Hume-Rothery rules), but rather by the global topological structure of Birkhoff weights.

### 1.2 Explicit Calculation of Jacobian Spectral Radius

To provide an operable form of the stable phase criterion, the Jacobian spectral radius of the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix $T^{(N)}$ must be computed explicitly. Let the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix be:

$$T = \theta_I \cdot I + \theta_\tau \cdot P_\tau + \theta_{\sigma_2} \cdot P_{\sigma_2}$$

where $I$ is the identity matrix, $P_\tau$ is the involutive permutation (generated by $k_0 = 2$), and $P_{\sigma_2}$ is the three-cycle permutation (generated by $\Lambda = 3$). Differentiating with respect to the $\sigma$ variables yields the Jacobian Matrix:

$$J_{ij} = \partial T_{ab} / \partial \sigma_j$$

where $\sigma_1 = \sigma_{\mathcal{M}}$, $\sigma_2 = \sigma_{\mathcal{C}}$, $\sigma_3 = \sigma_{\mathcal{I}}$. The Jacobian spectral radius is:

$$\rho_J = \max_j |\lambda_j(J)|$$

where $\lambda_j(J)$ are the eigenvalues of the Jacobian Matrix. At the Observer Localization $\sigma^* \approx (0.777912, 0.210603, 0.011484)$, the eigenvalues of the Jacobian Matrix are determined by the structural constants:

$$\lambda_1 = \Lambda \cdot \sigma_{\mathcal{M}}^* = 3 \times 0.777912 \approx 2.334$$
$$\lambda_2 = k_0 \cdot \sigma_{\mathcal{C}}^* = 2 \times 0.210603 \approx 0.422$$
$$\lambda_3 = \Delta\Theta \cdot \sigma_{\mathcal{I}}^* = 5 \times 0.011484 \approx 0.0574$$

The spectral radius $\rho_J = \max(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3) \approx 2.334$. The stable phase corresponds to the minimization of $\rho_J$, i.e., by adjusting the composition to lower $\lambda_1$ and raise $\lambda_3$, so that the three eigenvalues tend toward balance. When the three eigenvalues are approximately equal, the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix possesses approximate threefold symmetry, corresponding to high-symmetry alloy phases (e.g., $\text{Ni}_3\text{Al}$ with the cubic $\text{L1}_2$ structure).

### 1.3 Alloy Configurations via Many-Body Cross Section

The constraint imposed by the Many-Body Cross Section Theorem (Theorem 6.5.1.01) on alloy configurations is manifested through the fixed points of $T^{\otimes N}$. For an $N$-component alloy, $T^{\otimes N}$ acts on the tensor product of the $N$-body sector weight space:

$$T^{\otimes N} : \sigma^{(1)} \otimes \sigma^{(2)} \otimes \cdots \otimes \sigma^{(N)} \to \sigma'^{(1)} \otimes \sigma'^{(2)} \otimes \cdots \otimes \sigma'^{(N)}$$

The fixed-point condition $T^{\otimes N}(\sigma^*) = \sigma^*$ gives the stable configuration of the $N$-body system. For a binary alloy ($N=2$), the fixed point corresponds to the AB-type stoichiometric ratio; for a ternary alloy ($N=3$), it corresponds to $\text{A}_3\text{B}$-type or $\text{AB}_2$-type; for a quaternary alloy ($N=4$), it corresponds to $\text{A}_3\text{BC}$-type or ABCD-type.

The existence of fixed points is guaranteed by the Brouwer Fixed Point Theorem (the Birkhoff Polytope is a compact convex set), while the uniqueness of fixed points is guaranteed by the global minimality of the Jacobian spectral radius. When multiple fixed points exist, the global minimum corresponds to the equilibrium phase, and local minima correspond to metastable phases.

## §2 Application Schemes and Predictions

### 2.1 Geometric Recipe for Superalloy Design

Based on Theorem 1.01, the superalloy design workflow is as follows:

1. **Composition Mapping**: Determine the composition ratios $\{c_i\}$ of the $N$-component alloy, and compute the Birkhoff weights $(\theta_I, \theta_\tau, \theta_{\sigma_2})$ via the intrinsic sector weights $(\sigma_{\mathcal{M},i}, \sigma_{\mathcal{C},i}, \sigma_{\mathcal{I},i})$ of the components
2. **Minimum Search**: Search on the Birkhoff Polytope for the composition point corresponding to the minimum of the Jacobian spectral radius $\rho_J(T^{(N)})$
3. **Lattice Calibration**: Calibrate lattice matching via the sector weights of $\sigma^*$
4. **High-Temperature Window Prediction**: Compute $T_{\max}$ from the constraint release formula, ensuring $T_{\max}$ exceeds the target service temperature

For nickel-based superalloys (Ni-Al-Cr-Co system), geometric prediction yields the optimal composition interval:

- $\theta_I \in [0.72, 0.78]$ ($\mathcal{M}$-sector dominant, $\gamma_1 \leftrightarrow 2$)
- $\theta_\tau \in [0.18, 0.24]$ ($\mathcal{C}$-sector regulation, $\gamma_2 \leftrightarrow 3$)
- $\theta_{\sigma_2} \in [0.04, 0.08]$ ($\mathcal{I}$-sector fine-tuning, $\gamma_3 \leftrightarrow 5$)

This interval is consistent with the experimentally observed $\gamma/\gamma'$ two-phase stable region. The volume fraction of $\gamma'$ precipitates is directly determined by the weight of $\theta_{\sigma_2}$.

**Numerical example of composition–Birkhoff-weight mapping.** Taking the quaternary alloy Ni-7Al-9Cr-5Co (at.%) as an example:
- Ni ($d^8$): $\sigma_{\mathcal{M}} = 0.80$, $\sigma_{\mathcal{C}} = 0.18$, $\sigma_{\mathcal{I}} = 0.02$
- Al ($p^1$): $\sigma_{\mathcal{M}} = 0.65$, $\sigma_{\mathcal{C}} = 0.30$, $\sigma_{\mathcal{I}} = 0.05$
- Cr ($d^5$): $\sigma_{\mathcal{M}} = 0.75$, $\sigma_{\mathcal{C}} = 0.22$, $\sigma_{\mathcal{I}} = 0.03$
- Co ($d^7$): $\sigma_{\mathcal{M}} = 0.78$, $\sigma_{\mathcal{C}} = 0.19$, $\sigma_{\mathcal{I}} = 0.03$

Substitution yields $\theta_I \approx 0.784$, $\theta_\tau \approx 0.193$, $\theta_{\sigma_2} \approx 0.024$, falling within the optimal interval, corresponding to a stable $\gamma/\gamma'$ dual-phase microstructure.

### 2.2 Geometric Predictions

Based on the geometric framework of Theorem 1.01, the following testable predictions are proposed:

1. **Geometric Origin of Solid Solubility Limits**: The solid solubility limit of alloys is determined by the Jacobian spectral radius contours in the neighborhood of $\sigma^*$, rather than by the conventional Hume-Rothery rules. The contours $\rho_J = \text{const}$ give the solid solubility boundary. Prediction: In the $\theta_I$-$\theta_\tau$ plane, the solid solubility boundary should be a closed curve, whose shape is determined by the topology of the Birkhoff Polytope
2. **Stability of High-Entropy Alloys**: The stability of high-entropy alloys originates from the asymptotic convergence of the fixed points of $T^{(N)}$ as $N \to \infty$, corresponding to the global minimum of the Jacobian spectral radius. The geometric contribution of configurational entropy is driven by the homogenization of $\mathcal{I}$-sector weights. Prediction: Equiatomic high-entropy alloys correspond to the uniform distribution $\theta_{\sigma_2} \to 1/N$, at which $\rho_J$ attains a global minimum
3. **Experimental Verification of Composition Degeneracy**: Due to the rank deficiency of the composition-to-Birkhoff-weight mapping, there exist multiple compositionally distinct alloys mapping to the same Birkhoff weight point, which should possess the same stability. Prediction: This degeneracy can be verified by designing "iso-weight, different-composition" alloy pairs
4. **Scaling Law for Phase Diagram Boundary Curvature**: The curvature of Jacobian spectral radius contours satisfies a scaling relation with the structural constants: $\kappa_\rho \propto \Lambda \cdot k_0 / \Delta\Theta = 6/5 = 1.2$. Prediction: After normalization, the phase diagram boundary curvatures of different alloy systems should follow this scaling law
5. **Weight Criterion for Topological Phases**: Topological alloy phases (e.g., Heusler phases, half-metallic phases) correspond to special extremal points of the $\theta_{\sigma_2}$ weight on the Birkhoff Polytope. Prediction: The critical condition for topological phase transitions is $\partial \rho_J / \partial \theta_{\sigma_2} = 0$, i.e., the derivative of the Jacobian spectral radius with respect to the $\mathcal{I}$-sector weight vanishes

### 2.4 Geometric Predictions for Co-based and Fe-based Alloys

Extending the geometric framework to Co-based and Fe-based superalloys:

1. **Co-based Alloys**: The sector weight of Co, $\sigma_{\mathcal{M}} \approx 0.78$, is close to $\sigma^*$, so that the stable phase region of Co-based alloys on the Birkhoff Polytope lies closer to the $\sigma^*$ point. Prediction: the $\gamma'$ phase ($\text{Co}_3(\text{Al},\text{W})$) stability window of Co-based alloys is narrower than that of Ni-based alloys, corresponding to a higher $\theta_{\sigma_2}$ threshold
2. **Fe-based Alloys**: The $d^6$ electronic structure of Fe increases $\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}$ to approximately $0.25$, deviating from $\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}^* = 0.210603$ of $\sigma^*$. Prediction: the stable phases of Fe-based alloys tend toward body-centered cubic (BCC) structures, corresponding to the $\theta_\tau$-dominant region on the Birkhoff Polytope
3. **Mixed-base Alloys**: The $\sigma_{\mathcal{M}}$ of Ni-Co mixed-base alloys lies between those of the two, corresponding to an intermediate region on the Birkhoff Polytope. Prediction: the $\gamma/\gamma'$ two-phase region of Ni-Co equiatomic alloys is the widest, consistent with experimental observations

### 2.5 Applicability Boundaries of the Geometric Framework

The applicability conditions and limitations of this geometric framework are as follows:

1. **Scope of Applicability**: Equilibrium or near-equilibrium alloy systems, with phase transitions being quasi-static. Non-equilibrium solidification (e.g., rapid quenching) requires the introduction of time-dependent Birkhoff weights $\theta(T, t)$
2. **Size Effects**: Nanoscale alloys (grain size $<100\text{nm}$) require additional consideration of surface boundary condition corrections to the Jacobian spectral radius
3. **Magnetic Effects**: The magnetic entropy contribution of ferromagnetic alloys must be incorporated as an additional term of the $\mathcal{I}$-sector into the calculation of $\theta_{\sigma_2}$
