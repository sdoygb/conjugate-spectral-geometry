Graph-Theoretic Definition of Information Field Cumulants

Prerequisites: Theorem 5.1.1.01 (Algebraic Connectivity), Theorem 7.13.3.05 (Birkhoff Mixing), Theorem 7.13.4.01 (Hard-Direction Lyapunov Contraction)

Abstract

This article provides a graph-theoretic precise definition of information field cumulants, filling G1 in the proof framework of “information field cumulant factorization.” The definition has two levels: the base Clifford graph (\(N_{\text{info}}=16\)) and the parameter-space fine-grained graph (\(N_{\text{info}}\approx 9.024\times 10^{22}\)). The two levels share the same algebraic structure, differing only in node count. Core result: the upper bound of the k-th order cumulant is jointly controlled by the Birkhoff spectral gap \(\lambda_1^{\text{eff}}\) and the hard-direction Lyapunov exponent \(\Lambda_\xi\).

§1 Graph Structure and Dynamics

1.1 Information Field Graph

Definition 1.1 (Information Field Graph): The information field graph \(G_{\mathcal{I}} = (V, E, w)\) is a weighted directed graph, where:

  • \(V = \{1, 2, \ldots, N_{\text{info}}\}\) is the set of nodes
  • \(E \subseteq V \times V\) is the set of edges, \(e_{ij} \in E\) iff \(T_{ij} > 0\)
  • \(w: E \to (0, 1]\) is the edge weight, \(w(e_{ij}) = T_{ij}\)

The Birkhoff mixing matrix \(T\) is an \(N_{\text{info}} \times N_{\text{info}}\) doubly stochastic matrix:

\[\sum_{j} T_{ij} = \sum_{i} T_{ij} = 1, \quad T_{ij} \geq 0.\]

The graph Laplacian is \(L_{\mathcal{I}} = I - T\) (Theorem 5.1.1.01).

Two-Level Implementation:

Level \(N_{\text{info}}\) Origin Construction of \(T\)
Base level (Clifford algebra) 16 Cl(7) ≅ Mat(8,ℝ) ⊕ Mat(8,ℝ), ρ₇ × k₀ = 8 × 2 \(3 \times 3\) core block of \(T\) + intra-sector degeneracy (Theorem 3.3.1.01)
Parameter space level (continuum limit) ≈ 9.024 × 10²² Discretization of sector parameter space Σ ≈ S² (Theorem 3.2.2.01) Fibration structure: at each point, replicate the base-level T; points are coupled by the parameter space graph Laplacian

The algebraic structure of the two levels is identical — the eigenvalues of \(T\), \(\lambda_1=1\), \(\lambda_{2,3}=0.678\pm 0.181i\), and the corresponding spectral gap \(\lambda_1^{\text{eff}}=391.05\text{ rad}^{-2}\), are invariant — the only differences lie in matrix dimension and degeneracy.

1.2 Information Density Distribution

Definition 1.2 (Information Density Distribution): At time \(\tau\) (causal advance steps), the information density distribution on the information field graph is a probability vector:

\[\rho_\tau \in \Delta^{N_{\text{info}}-1} = \left\{ \rho \in \mathbb{R}_+^{N_{\text{info}}} \;\middle|\; \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{info}}} \rho_i = 1 \right\}.\]

\(\rho_\tau(i)\) represents the share of information carried by node \(i\) at time \(\tau\). The distribution evolves with causal advance:

\[\rho_{\tau+1} = T \rho_\tau, \quad \text{or in continuous form } \partial_\tau \rho = -L_{\mathcal{I}} \rho.\]

Steady-state distribution (\(\tau \to \infty\)): By the Perron-Frobenius theorem, \(\rho_\infty(i) = 1/N_{\text{info}}\) (uniform distribution).

§2 Information Field Cumulants

2.1 Moments and Joint Expectations

Definition 2.1 (k-point joint density): Under the steady-state causal circulation \(T_{ss}\), the k-point joint information density of \(k\) nodes \((i_1, \ldots, i_k) \in V^k\) is:

\[\rho^{(k)}_\tau(i_1, \ldots, i_k) = \mathbb{E}_{T_{ss}^\tau}\left[ \prod_{j=1}^{k} \delta_{X_j, i_j} \right],\]

where \(X_j\) is the node-position random variable of the \(j\)-th information trajectory, and \(\delta\) is the Kronecker delta. The joint density is normalized with respect to each of the \(k\) coordinates:

\[\sum_{i_1, \ldots, i_k} \rho^{(k)}_\tau(i_1, \ldots, i_k) = 1.\]

Definition 2.2 (k-th order joint moment): For observation functions \(f_1, \ldots, f_k: V \to \mathbb{R}\), the k-th order joint moment is:

\[M_k(f_1, \ldots, f_k; \tau) = \mathbb{E}_{T_{ss}^\tau}\left[ \prod_{j=1}^{k} f_j(X_j) \right] = \sum_{i_1, \ldots, i_k} \left( \prod_{j=1}^{k} f_j(i_j) \right) \rho^{(k)}_\tau(i_1, \ldots, i_k).\]

When \(f_j = \delta_{i_j}\) (indicator functions), \(M_k\) reduces to \(\rho^{(k)}_\tau(i_1, \ldots, i_k)\).

2.2 Combinatorial Definition of Cumulants

Definition 2.3 (k-th order information field cumulant): The k-th order information field cumulant \(C_k\) is defined by the standard combinatorial formula for cumulants. For \(k\) nodes \((i_1, \ldots, i_k)\):

\[C_k(i_1, \ldots, i_k; \tau) = \sum_{\pi \in \Pi_k} (-1)^{|\pi|-1} (|\pi|-1)! \prod_{B \in \pi} \rho^{(|B|)}_\tau(\{i_j\}_{j \in B}),\]

where:

  • \(\Pi_k\) is the set of all partitions of \(\{1, 2, \ldots, k\}\)
  • \(\pi\) is a partition, \(|\pi|\) is the number of blocks in the partition
  • \(B \in \pi\) is a block in the partition, \(|B|\) is the size of that block
  • \(\rho^{(|B|)}_\tau(\{i_j\}_{j \in B})\) is the \(|B|\)-point joint density corresponding to the nodes in block \(B\)

Equivalent form (generating function definition):

\[C_k(i_1, \ldots, i_k; \tau) = \left. \frac{\partial^k}{\partial t_1 \cdots \partial t_k} \log \mathbb{E}_{T_{ss}^\tau}\left[ \exp\left( \sum_{j=1}^{k} t_j \delta_{X_j, i_j} \right) \right] \right|_{t_1 = \cdots = t_k = 0}.\]

Physical interpretation: \(C_k\) measures the residual in the information density of \(k\) nodes that cannot be explained by any lower-order correlations. For example:

  • \(C_1(i) = \rho_\tau(i)\) — the first-order cumulant is simply the marginal distribution itself
  • \(C_2(i, j) = \rho^{(2)}_\tau(i, j) - \rho_\tau(i) \rho_\tau(j)\) — the second-order cumulant is the covariance (correlation beyond the independent product)
  • \(C_3(i, j, k)\) — the third-order cumulant is the residual after subtracting all pairwise correlations and the three-body independent product. If \(C_3 = 0\), then three-body correlations are completely explained by two-body correlations.

2.3 Factorization Condition

Definition 2.4 (k-th order factorization): The information field at time \(\tau\) is called k-th order factorized if, for all \(m \geq k\):

\[C_m(i_1, \ldots, i_m; \tau) = 0, \quad \forall (i_1, \ldots, i_m) \in V^m.\]

Equivalently, the joint density is completely determined by correlations of order at most \(k-1\) — i.e., \(\rho^{(m)}_\tau\) (for \(m \geq k\)) can be expressed via products and sums of lower-order joint densities (\(\leq k-1\)), as given by the combinatorial inversion formula of cumulants.

Factorization hierarchy: - First-order factorization (\(k=2\), i.e., \(C_m = 0\) for all \(m \geq 2\)): all cumulants vanish; the joint density completely factorizes into the product of marginals \(\rho^{(m)}_\tau = \prod \rho_\tau\). This corresponds to the “molecular chaos hypothesis” in statistical mechanics — many-body probabilities are completely determined by single-body probabilities. - Second-order factorization (\(k=3\), i.e., \(C_m = 0\) for all \(m \geq 3\)): third- and higher-order cumulants vanish, but pairwise correlations \(C_2\) may be nonzero. The joint density is determined by first- and second-order correlations — a weaker condition than molecular chaos.

Note: In Definition 2.3, the arguments of \(C_k\) are graph node labels \((i_1, \ldots, i_k)\). At the parameter space level (\(N_{\text{info}} \approx 10^{22}\)), node labels can be mapped to continuous coordinates \(x \in \Sigma \approx S^2\), so that \(C_k\) reduces to connected Green’s functions in continuum field theory. This correspondence is guaranteed by Assumption A.1 (graph Laplacian \(\to\) Laplace-Beltrami), but the definition of \(C_k\) itself does not require A.1 — it is defined purely on the graph \(G_{\mathcal{I}}\).

§3 Connection to Birkhoff Mixing

3.1 Mixing ⇒ Two-Point Correlation Decay

Proposition 5.6.3.01 (Mixing decay of two-point cumulant): Under the steady state \(T_{ss}\), for any two observation functions \(f, g: V \to \mathbb{R}\), the two-point cumulant satisfies:

\[|C_2(f, g; \tau)| = \left| \mathbb{E}_{T_{ss}^\tau}[f(X) g(Y)] - \mathbb{E}[f] \mathbb{E}[g] \right| \leq \|f\| \cdot \|g\| \cdot e^{-\lambda_1^{\text{eff}} \tau},\]

where \(\|f\| = \max_i |f(i)|\), and \(\lambda_1^{\text{eff}} = 391.05\text{ rad}^{-2}\) is the effective spectral gap (Theorem 7.13.3.05).

Proof (outline): By Birkhoff mixing (Theorem 7.13.3.05), the action of \(T_{ss}\) on the state space satisfies: for any square-integrable functions \(f, g\),

\[\left| \langle f(T_{ss}^\tau x) g(x) \rangle_{\mu_{ss}} - \langle f \rangle \langle g \rangle \right| \leq \|f\|_2 \|g\|_2 \cdot e^{-\lambda_1^{\text{eff}} \tau}.\]

Taking \(f = \delta_i - 1/N_{\text{info}}\), \(g = \delta_j - 1/N_{\text{info}}\) yields the node version. \(\|f\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i f(i)^2 \mu_{ss}(i)}\), which is equivalent to the \(\sup\) norm on the space of bounded functions (up to a constant factor). ∎

3.2 Graph-Theoretic Recursion for Higher-Order Cumulants

Lemma 5.6.3.02 (Graph-theoretic expansion of cumulants): The k-th order cumulant \(C_k(i_1, \ldots, i_k; \tau)\) can be expanded as a weighted sum over all connected k-point paths on the graph:

\[C_k(i_1, \ldots, i_k; \tau) = \sum_{\substack{\gamma: \text{connected k-path} \\ \text{connecting } i_1, \ldots, i_k}} w(\gamma) \cdot \prod_{e \in \gamma} T_{ss}(e)^\tau,\]

where a connected k-path \(\gamma\) is a subgraph of \(G_{\mathcal{I}}\) whose vertex set is exactly \(\{i_1, \ldots, i_k\}\) and is connected; \(w(\gamma)\) is a combinatorial weight; and \(T_{ss}(e)^\tau\) is the transition probability along edge \(e\) under \(\tau\) steps of causal advance.

Explanation: This expansion is a direct consequence of the combinatorial definition of cumulants (Definition 2.3) — the joint density \(\rho^{(|B|)}_\tau\) is itself given by the product of transition probabilities on the graph, and the partition sum formula for cumulants automatically filters out the “connected contribution” (disconnected parts are canceled by lower-order terms). This property is known in statistical mechanics as the “cluster decomposition” of cumulants, and it continues to hold on graphs.

§4 Basic Properties of Cumulants

4.1 Identities

Proposition 5.6.4.01 (Basic properties of cumulants):

  1. Multilinearity: \(C_k\) is multilinear in each argument — if \(f_j = a g_j + b h_j\), then \(C_k\) decomposes linearly
  2. Translation invariance: \(C_k(f_1 + c_1, \ldots, f_k + c_k) = C_k(f_1, \ldots, f_k)\) (\(k \geq 2\))
  3. Independence ⇔ vanishing: If \(\{X_1, \ldots, X_k\}\) can be partitioned into two independent subsets, then \(C_k = 0\)
  4. Scaling: \(C_k(a_1 f_1, \ldots, a_k f_k) = \left( \prod_{j=1}^{k} a_j \right) C_k(f_1, \ldots, f_k)\)
  5. Symmetry: \(C_k\) is symmetric in its arguments

These are standard properties inherited directly from the combinatorial definition of cumulants, and they continue to hold on graphs — because they depend only on the algebraic structure of the expectation operator, not on the specific nature of the underlying space.

4.2 Steady-State Limit

Proposition 5.6.4.02 (Trivialization of steady-state cumulants): As \(\tau \to \infty\), all cumulants of order \(k \geq 2\) tend to zero:

\[\lim_{\tau \to \infty} C_k(i_1, \ldots, i_k; \tau) = 0, \quad \forall k \geq 2.\]

Proof: The steady-state distribution \(\rho_\infty(i) = 1/N_{\text{info}}\) is uniform. By Birkhoff double stochasticity, \(T_{ss}^\infty\) projects any initial distribution onto the uniform distribution, and this projection is a rank-1 operator. The joint density \(\rho^{(k)}_\infty(i_1, \ldots, i_k) = 1/N_{\text{info}}^k = \prod_{j=1}^{k} \rho_\infty(i_j)\) factorizes completely. By Definition 2.3, complete factorization ⇒ all \(C_k = 0\) (\(k \geq 2\)). ∎

§5 Unification of the Two-Level Structure

Definition 5.1 (Fibration lift of information field cumulants): At the parameter space level (\(N_{\text{info}} \approx 9.024 \times 10^{22}\)), node labels \(i\) map to parameter space coordinates \(x_i \in \Sigma \approx S^2\). The cumulants \(C_k\) admit a continuum limit form:

\[c_k(x_1, \ldots, x_k; \tau) = \lim_{N_{\text{info}} \to \infty} N_{\text{info}}^k \cdot C_k(i(x_1), \ldots, i(x_k); \tau),\]

where \(i(x)\) is the graph node label to which \(x\) belongs. The existence of this limit is guaranteed by Assumption A.1 (Theorem 3.2.2.01, Appendix B).

Note: The factor \(N_{\text{info}}^k\) comes from the Jacobian mapping discrete probability density to continuous density — in the continuum limit, single-point probability \(\rho(i) \sim 1/N_{\text{info}}\), so that \(c_k\) as a continuous density is \(O(1)\). However, Definition 2.3 (discrete cumulant) itself does not depend on this limit — it can be computed directly for finite \(N_{\text{info}}\).
