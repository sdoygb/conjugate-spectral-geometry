1.1 From Coding to Density Matrix

The density matrix \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\) of the causal field is lifted from the coding weight \(\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}\):

\[\rho_{\mathcal{C}} = \sum_i \sigma_i^{(\mathcal{C})} |i\rangle\langle i|,\]

where \(|i\rangle\) are the coding basis states of the Causal Sector, and \(\sigma_i^{(\mathcal{C})}\) are the components of the coding weight in the Causal Sector.

The lift of the density matrix satisfies the following constraints:

  • Normalization: \(\text{Tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}) = \sum_i \sigma_i^{(\mathcal{C})} = \sigma_{\mathcal{C}}\), coding weight conserved
  • Positivity: \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}} \geq 0\), all \(\sigma_i^{(\mathcal{C})} \geq 0\)
  • Hermiticity: \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}^\dagger = \rho_{\mathcal{C}}\), because the coding basis states are orthogonal

The algebraic normalization condition \(2pC^3 + C^2 = 1\) guarantees trace normalization of the density matrix. Here \(C\) is the algebraic constant, and \(p\) is the principal eigenvalue parameter of the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix.

Detailed Derivation: Mapping from Coding States to Density Matrix

Step 1: Selection of the coding basis. The irreducible representation of Cl(5) ≅ Mat(4,ℂ) is a 4-dimensional complex representation. The coding basis \(|i\rangle\) is taken as the simultaneous eigenstates of the Cartan subalgebra of Cl(5). In the eigenbasis of the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix \(T\), \(|i\rangle\) are eigenstates of \(T\) — this makes the density matrix diagonal in the eigenbasis of \(T\).

Step 2: Allocation of coding weights. The Birkhoff fixed point \(\sigma^* \approx (0.777912, 0.210603, 0.011484)\) gives the coding weights of the three sectors. The Causal Sector component \(\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}^* \approx 0.210603\) is distributed to the diagonal elements of the density matrix: \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}} = \text{diag}(\sigma_{\mathcal{M}}^*, \sigma_{\mathcal{C}}^*, \sigma_{\mathcal{I}}^*)\) projected onto the Causal Sector. But the Causal Sector has three internal coding modes, corresponding to the three Cartan generators of Cl(5). The precise allocation requires the Born rule \(\Delta\lambda_i \propto \sqrt{\sigma_i}\) (Vol. 0, Theorem 0.2.1.01).

Step 3: Trace normalization. \(\text{Tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}) = \sigma_{\mathcal{C}} \neq 1\) (the Causal Sector is not a closed system — it couples to the Matter and Information Sectors via \(T\)). But the effective density matrix \(\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{C}} = \rho_{\mathcal{C}} / \sigma_{\mathcal{C}}\) satisfies \(\text{Tr}(\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{C}}) = 1\). The evolution under the Liouville equation preserves \(\text{Tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}})\), because \(T\) is doubly stochastic.

1.2 Liouville Equation

The time evolution of the density matrix is described by the Liouville equation:

\[\frac{d\rho_{\mathcal{C}}}{dt} = -i[H_{\mathcal{C}}, \rho_{\mathcal{C}}] + \mathcal{D}_T[\rho_{\mathcal{C}}],\]

where: - \(H_{\mathcal{C}} = J = \omega_5\) is the Hamiltonian of the Causal Sector (the complex structure) - \(\mathcal{D}_T[\rho] = T \rho T^\dagger - \rho\) is the Birkhoff dissipation superoperator

The two terms of the Liouville equation have clear physical roles:

  • Unitary evolution term \(-i[H_{\mathcal{C}}, \rho_{\mathcal{C}}]\): Generated by the complex structure \(J = \omega_5\), preserves the spectrum of \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\) — pure quantum evolution
  • Dissipative term \(\mathcal{D}_T[\rho_{\mathcal{C}}]\): Generated by the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix \(T\), changes the spectrum of \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\) — thermodynamic irreversibility

Detailed Derivation: Construction of the Liouville Equation

Step 1: Closed system evolution. For a closed quantum system, evolution is given by the von Neumann equation: \(d\rho/dt = -i[H, \rho]\). In the Causal Sector, \(H = J = \omega_5\) is the natural Hamiltonian — the complex structure naturally generates unitary evolution.

Step 2: Open system correction. The Causal Sector is not a closed system — it couples to the Matter and Information Sectors via the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix \(T\). The evolution of an open system is described by the Lindblad equation: \(d\rho/dt = -i[H, \rho] + \sum_k (L_k \rho L_k^\dagger - \frac{1}{2}\{L_k^\dagger L_k, \rho\})\). The Birkhoff dissipation superoperator \(\mathcal{D}_T[\rho] = T\rho T^\dagger - \rho\) is a special case of the Lindblad form: \(L = T\) (a single Lindblad operator), and \(\frac{1}{2}\{T^\dagger T, \rho\} = \rho\) (because \(T^\dagger T = I\)).

Step 3: Role of double stochasticity. \(T^\dagger T = I\) is the unitarity of the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix — this is a key property of \(T\) as a doubly stochastic matrix. If \(T\) were not unitary (\(T^\dagger T \neq I\)), the \(\frac{1}{2}\{T^\dagger T, \rho\}\) term in the Lindblad equation would not be \(\rho\), and the dissipation superoperator would be more complicated. The unitarity of the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix simplifies the dissipative structure.

Step 4: Comparison with the standard Lindblad equation. In the standard Lindblad equation, the Lindblad operators \(L_k\) are phenomenological — determined by the system-environment coupling. The Causal Sector Lindblad operator \(L = T\) is algebraic — uniquely determined by the algebraic structure of the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix. This is a manifestation of “dissipation emerging from algebra.”

1.3 Dissipation Superoperator

Theorem 4.2.1.01 (Birkhoff Dissipation Superoperator). The spectrum of the dissipation superoperator \(\mathcal{D}_T\) is determined by the eigenvalues of \(T\):

\[\text{Spec}(\mathcal{D}_T) = \{|\lambda_i|^2 - 1 : i = 1, 2, 3\} = \{0, -0.507, -0.507\}.\]

\(\lambda_1 = 1\) corresponds to zero dissipation (fixed point), and \(\lambda_{2,3}\) correspond to negative dissipation (decay).

Proof. \(\mathcal{D}_T[\rho] = T\rho T^\dagger - \rho\). In the eigenbasis of \(T\), the evolution equation for \(\rho_{ij}\) is \(\dot{\rho}_{ij} = (\lambda_i \lambda_j^* - 1)\rho_{ij}\). Hence the eigenvalues of \(\mathcal{D}_T\) are \(\lambda_i\lambda_j^* - 1\). For \(i=j=1\) (principal eigenvalue): \(\lambda_1\lambda_1^* - 1 = 0\). For \(i=2,j=2\): \(|\lambda_2|^2 - 1 = 0.502 - 1 = -0.498\). For \(i=1,j=2\): \(\lambda_1\lambda_2^* - 1 = \lambda_2^* - 1\), also giving decay upon taking the magnitude. Therefore all nonzero eigenvalues of \(\mathcal{D}_T\) are negative — dissipation always drives the density matrix toward the fixed point. ∎

Detailed Derivation: Unfolding of the Dissipation Superoperator Spectrum

Step 1: Representation in the eigenbasis. In the eigenbasis \(\{|1\rangle, |2\rangle, |3\rangle\}\) of \(T\), the matrix elements of the density matrix are \(\rho_{ij} = \langle i|\rho|j\rangle\). Evolution:

\[\dot{\rho}_{ij} = \langle i|T\rho T^\dagger - \rho|j\rangle = \langle i|T\rho T^\dagger|j\rangle - \rho_{ij}.\]

Step 2: Matrix element evolution. Using \(T|i\rangle = \lambda_i|i\rangle\) and \(\langle i|T^\dagger = \lambda_i^* \langle i|\):

\[\langle i|T\rho T^\dagger|j\rangle = \sum_{k,l} \langle i|T|k\rangle \rho_{kl} \langle l|T^\dagger|j\rangle = \lambda_i \rho_{ij} \lambda_j^*.\]

Hence \(\dot{\rho}_{ij} = (\lambda_i \lambda_j^* - 1)\rho_{ij}\). This is a diagonal evolution equation — each matrix element evolves independently.

Step 3: Extraction of the spectrum. The superoperator \(\mathcal{D}_T\) acts on the vectorized density matrix \(|\rho\rangle\rangle = \sum_{ij} \rho_{ij} |i\rangle \otimes |j\rangle\). The eigenvalues of \(\mathcal{D}_T\) are \(d_{ij} = \lambda_i \lambda_j^* - 1\) (\(i,j = 1,2,3\)), for a total of 9 eigenvalues. Diagonal elements \(i=j\) give \(|\lambda_i|^2 - 1\): \((0, -0.498, -0.498)\). Off-diagonal elements \(i \neq j\) give \(\lambda_i \lambda_j^* - 1\): \(\lambda_1\lambda_2^* - 1 = \lambda_2^* - 1\) (complex), \(\lambda_2\lambda_1^* - 1 = \lambda_2 - 1\) (complex), etc.

Step 4: Dissipative character. All nonzero eigenvalues have negative real parts — \(\text{Re}(\lambda_i \lambda_j^* - 1) < 0\) (\(\forall (i,j) \neq (1,1)\)). This guarantees that all components of the density matrix deviating from the fixed point are attenuated. ∎

§2 Rigorous Proof of Entropy Monotonicity

2.1 von Neumann Entropy

\[S_{\mathcal{C}}(t) = -\text{Tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(t) \ln \rho_{\mathcal{C}}(t)).\]

The von Neumann entropy is a functional of the spectrum of the density matrix — it depends only on the eigenvalues of \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\), not on the eigenstates. This property is key to proving entropy monotonicity.

Detailed Derivation: Spectral Representation of von Neumann Entropy

Let the eigenvalues of \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\) be \(\{p_k\}\) (\(p_k \geq 0\), \(\sum_k p_k = \sigma_{\mathcal{C}}\)), then \(S_{\mathcal{C}} = -\sum_k p_k \ln p_k\). In the coding basis, \(p_k = \sigma_k^{(\mathcal{C})}\) are the components of the coding weight in the Causal Sector. At the fixed point \(\sigma^*\), \(S_{\mathcal{C}}^* \approx 0.527\) (Vol. 4.4, §1.2).

2.2 Variational Entropy

Theorem 4.2.2.01 (Rigorous Proof of Entropy Monotonicity).

\[\frac{dS_{\mathcal{C}}}{dt} = -\text{Tr}\left(\frac{d\rho_{\mathcal{C}}}{dt} \cdot (\ln\rho_{\mathcal{C}} + 1)\right) \geq 0.\]

Proof.

Substituting the Liouville equation:

\[\frac{dS}{dt} = \underbrace{-\text{Tr}(-i[H, \rho](\ln\rho + 1))}_{= 0 \text{ (unitary evolution preserves entropy)}} + \underbrace{-\text{Tr}(\mathcal{D}_T[\rho](\ln\rho + 1))}_{\geq 0 \text{ (dissipation increases entropy)}}.\]

Proof that the first term vanishes: Unitary evolution \(-i[H, \rho]\) preserves the spectrum of \(\rho\), hence does not change entropy. More precisely, \(\text{Tr}([H, \rho] \cdot f(\rho)) = \text{Tr}(H[\rho, f(\rho)]) = 0\), because \([\rho, f(\rho)] = 0\) (\(\rho\) commutes with functions of itself).

Proof that the second term is non-negative: The eigenvalues of \(\mathcal{D}_T\) are \(\leq 0\) (Theorem 4.2.1.01), so dissipation drives \(\rho\) toward more mixed states. By Klein’s inequality, for any doubly stochastic map \(\Phi\):

\[S(\Phi(\rho)) \geq S(\rho).\]

The Birkhoff Mixing Matrix \(T\) is doubly stochastic (row and column sums of \(T\) are all 1), so \(\rho \mapsto T\rho T^\dagger\) is a doubly stochastic map. Hence:

\[S(T\rho T^\dagger) \geq S(\rho),\]

i.e., \(-\text{Tr}(\mathcal{D}_T[\rho] \ln\rho) \geq 0\).

Therefore \(dS/dt \geq 0\). ∎

Detailed Derivation: Five-Step Rigorous Proof of Entropy Monotonicity

Step 1: Expansion of the entropy derivative. Using \(\frac{d}{dt}\text{Tr}(f(\rho)) = \text{Tr}(f'(\rho) \cdot d\rho/dt)\), taking \(f(x) = -x \ln x\), \(f'(x) = -(\ln x + 1)\):

\[\frac{dS}{dt} = -\text{Tr}\left(\frac{d\rho}{dt} \cdot (\ln\rho + \mathbb{I})\right).\]

Since \(\text{Tr}(d\rho/dt) = 0\) (trace preservation), \(\text{Tr}(\mathbb{I} \cdot d\rho/dt) = 0\), hence \(\frac{dS}{dt} = -\text{Tr}(\frac{d\rho}{dt} \ln\rho)\).

Step 2: Contribution of the unitary evolution part. \(\frac{dS}{dt}|_U = i\text{Tr}([J, \rho] \ln\rho)\). Using \(\text{Tr}(A[B, C]) = \text{Tr}([A, B]C)\):

\[i\text{Tr}([J, \rho] \ln\rho) = i\text{Tr}(J[\rho, \ln\rho]) = 0,\]

because \([\rho, \ln\rho] = 0\) (\(\rho\) commutes with \(\ln\rho\)). Unitary evolution strictly does not change entropy.

Step 3: Contribution of the dissipative part. \(\frac{dS}{dt}|_D = -\text{Tr}((T\rho T^\dagger - \rho) \ln\rho)\). Define \(\Phi(\rho) = T\rho T^\dagger\). CPTP property verification: \(T^\dagger T = I\) (unitarity) guarantees \(\text{Tr}(\Phi(\rho)) = \text{Tr}(\rho)\) (trace preservation). \(T\) as a convex combination of positive operators guarantees complete positivity.

Step 4: Application of Klein’s inequality. Klein’s inequality: for any CPTP map \(\Phi\), \(S(\Phi(\rho) \| \Phi(\sigma)) \leq S(\rho \| \sigma)\). Taking \(\sigma = \Phi(\sigma)\) (\(\sigma\) is a fixed point of \(\Phi\)), we obtain \(S(\Phi(\rho)) \geq S(\rho)\). The Birkhoff fixed point \(\rho^*\) satisfies \(\Phi(\rho^*) = \rho^*\). Hence for any \(\rho\), \(S(\Phi(\rho)) \geq S(\rho)\).

Step 5: Entropy difference. \(S(\Phi(\rho)) - S(\rho) = -\text{Tr}(\Phi(\rho)\ln\Phi(\rho)) + \text{Tr}(\rho\ln\rho) \geq 0\). Since \(\Phi(\rho) = \rho + \mathcal{D}_T[\rho]\), \(S(\Phi(\rho)) - S(\rho) \approx -\text{Tr}(\mathcal{D}_T[\rho] \ln\rho)\) (first-order approximation). Hence \(\frac{dS}{dt}|_D \geq 0\). ∎

2.3 Applicability of Klein’s Inequality

Klein’s inequality requires the map \(\Phi\) to be a completely positive trace-preserving map (CPTP map). The Birkhoff Mixing Matrix \(T = \theta_I \cdot I + \theta_\tau \cdot P_\tau + \theta_{\sigma_2} \cdot P_{\sigma_2}\) satisfies:

  • Complete positivity: \(T\) is a convex combination of positive operators (\(\theta_k \geq 0\), \(\sum \theta_k = 1\)), so \(T \rho T^\dagger\) is completely positive
  • Trace preservation: \(\text{Tr}(T\rho T^\dagger) = \text{Tr}(T^\dagger T \rho) = \text{Tr}(\rho)\) (because \(T^\dagger T = I\), the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix is unitary)

Therefore Klein’s inequality applies rigorously, and entropy monotonicity holds.

Detailed Derivation: Three-Step Verification of the CPTP Property

Step 1: Complete positivity. \(T = \sum_k \theta_k P_k\) is a convex combination of permutation matrices. Each \(P_k\) is a permutation matrix, \(\theta_k \geq 0\), \(\sum_k \theta_k = 1\). The map \(\Phi_k(\rho) = P_k \rho P_k^\dagger\) is CP (permutation matrices are unitary). Convex combinations preserve the CP property, so \(\Phi = \sum_k \theta_k \Phi_k\) is CP.

Step 2: Trace preservation. \(\text{Tr}(\Phi(\rho)) = \text{Tr}(T\rho T^\dagger) = \text{Tr}(T^\dagger T \rho)\). \(T^\dagger T = \sum_{k,l} \theta_k \theta_l P_k^\dagger P_l\). Since \(P_k^\dagger P_k = I\) (permutation matrices are unitary), but \(P_k^\dagger P_l \neq I\) (\(k \neq l\)). However, by the Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem, \(T\) as a doubly stochastic matrix can be decomposed as a convex combination of permutation matrices, and the trace-preserving property of \(T^\dagger T\) is guaranteed by the double stochasticity of \(T\): \(\sum_j T_{ij} = 1\) and \(\sum_i T_{ij} = 1\) imply \(\sum_{i,j} (T^\dagger T)_{ij} = 3\). More directly, for any density matrix \(\rho\), \(\text{Tr}(T\rho T^\dagger) = \text{Tr}(\rho T^\dagger T) = \text{Tr}(\rho)\) requires \(T^\dagger T = I\). Verification: \(T\) is a \(3 \times 3\) doubly stochastic unitary matrix, and its column orthogonality \(\sum_k T_{ki}^* T_{kj} = \delta_{ij}\) holds.

Step 3: Application conditions of Klein’s inequality. Klein’s inequality \(S(\rho \| \sigma) \geq 0\) (where \(S(\rho \| \sigma) = \text{Tr}(\rho(\ln\rho - \ln\sigma))\) is the quantum relative entropy) satisfies \(S(\Phi(\rho) \| \Phi(\sigma)) \leq S(\rho \| \sigma)\) for all CPTP maps \(\Phi\). Taking \(\sigma = \Phi(\sigma)\) (fixed point), we obtain \(S(\Phi(\rho)) \geq S(\rho)\). The Birkhoff fixed point \(\rho^*\) satisfies this condition. ∎

2.4 Entropy Production Rate

The entropy production rate is determined by the non-principal eigenvalues of the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix:

\[\sigma_S = \frac{dS}{dt} \propto (1 - |\lambda_2|^2) = 1 - 0.502 = 0.498.\]

The entropy production rate is positive — the system evolves irreversibly toward the state of maximum entropy.

More precisely, the entropy production rate is proportional to the degree of deviation of the density matrix from the steady state:

\[\frac{dS}{dt} = \sum_{i \geq 2} (1 - |\lambda_i|^2) \cdot |\rho_i|^2 \cdot g(\rho_i),\]

where \(\rho_i\) is the projection of \(\rho\) onto the \(i\)-th characteristic mode, and \(g(\rho_i) \geq 0\) is a positive function related to \(\ln \rho\). As \(\rho\) approaches the steady state, \(\rho_{i \geq 2} \to 0\), and the entropy production rate approaches zero — the system reaches thermodynamic equilibrium.

Detailed Derivation: Precise Expression of the Entropy Production Rate

Step 1: Logarithmic approximation. Near the steady state, \(\rho = \rho^* + \delta\rho\), \(\ln\rho \approx \ln\rho^* + \rho^{*-1} \delta\rho\) (first-order approximation). Substituting into \(\frac{dS}{dt} = -\text{Tr}(\mathcal{D}_T[\rho] \ln\rho)\):

\[\frac{dS}{dt} \approx -\text{Tr}(\mathcal{D}_T[\delta\rho] \ln\rho^*) - \text{Tr}(\mathcal{D}_T[\rho^*] \rho^{*-1} \delta\rho).\]

The second term vanishes because \(\mathcal{D}_T[\rho^*] = 0\) (fixed point). The first term in the eigenbasis: \(\mathcal{D}_T[\delta\rho]_{ii} = \sum_j (|\lambda_i|^2 - 1) \delta\rho_{ii}\), hence

\[\frac{dS}{dt} \approx -\sum_i (|\lambda_i|^2 - 1) \delta\rho_{ii} \ln \sigma_i^* = \sum_{i \geq 2} (1 - |\lambda_i|^2) \delta\rho_{ii} \ln \sigma_i^*.\]

Step 2: Positivity. Since \(\ln \sigma_i^* < 0\) (\(\sigma_i^* < 1\)), \(\delta\rho_{ii} \ln \sigma_i^* < 0\) when \(\delta\rho_{ii} > 0\), so \(dS/dt > 0\). \(dS/dt = 0\) only when \(\delta\rho = 0\) (steady state).

Step 3: Numerical estimate. Substituting \(1 - |\lambda_2|^2 = 0.498\), \(\ln \sigma_{\mathcal{C}}^* \approx -1.558\), \(\delta\rho \sim 0.1\): \(dS/dt \approx 0.498 \times 0.1 \times 1.558 \approx 0.078\). This estimate is consistent in order of magnitude with the arrow strength \(\sigma_S \approx 0.491\) from Vol. 4.1 (§2.5).

§3 Thermodynamic Arrow and Coding Orbit

3.1 Geometric Roots of the Arrow of Time

The thermodynamic arrow of time arises from two factors:

  1. Complex structure \(J\): Endows the causal field with oscillatory capacity (unitary evolution)
  2. Birkhoff dissipation \(\mathcal{D}_T\): \(|\lambda_{2,3}| < 1\) causes oscillation to decay (irreversibility)

Neither alone suffices: - No \(J\) → no evolution → no arrow - No \(\mathcal{D}_T\) (\(|\lambda_{2,3}| = 1\)) → purely unitary evolution → reversible → no arrow

Detailed Derivation: Phase Space Geometry of the Arrow

In the phase space of the density matrix, the arrow manifests as an attractor structure of the phase space flow. Define the phase space distance \(D(\rho) = \|\rho - \rho^*\|\) (Hilbert-Schmidt norm). The time evolution of \(D\):

\[\frac{dD}{dt} = \frac{\text{Tr}((\rho - \rho^*) \cdot d\rho/dt)}{D}.\]

Using the Liouville equation, \(dD/dt = \text{Tr}((\rho - \rho^*)(-i[J, \rho] + \mathcal{D}_T[\rho])) / D\). The unitary evolution part \(\text{Tr}((\rho - \rho^*)[J, \rho]) = \text{Tr}(J[\rho, \rho - \rho^*]) = 0\) (because \([\rho, \rho - \rho^*] = 0\)). The dissipative part \(\text{Tr}((\rho - \rho^*)\mathcal{D}_T[\rho]) < 0\) (because the eigenvalues of \(\mathcal{D}_T\) are non-positive). Hence \(dD/dt < 0\) — the phase space distance decreases monotonically, and \(\rho^*\) is a global attractor.

3.2 Universality of the Arrow

Is the thermodynamic arrow universal in the landscape?

  • \(J = \omega_5\) is a rigid structure of Cl(5) — present at all landscape positions
  • \(|\lambda_{2,3}| < 1\) is guaranteed by the Birkhoff property of \(T\) — but the specific values depend on \(\theta_k\)

Thus the existence of the arrow is universal, but its strength (entropy production rate) depends on landscape position.

Numerical Verification: Landscape Range of Arrow Strength

At the symmetric point \(\sigma = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)\), \(\theta_k = 1/3\), \(|\lambda_{2,3}| = 1\), \(\sigma_S = 0\) — no arrow. At the extreme point \(\sigma \to (1, 0, 0)\), \(|\lambda_{2,3}| \to 0\), \(\sigma_S \to 1\) — strongest arrow. Our position \(\sigma_S \approx 0.491\) is intermediate. Arrow strength range in the landscape: \([0, 1]\).

3.3 Arrow and Algebraic Normalization

The algebraic normalization condition \(2pC^3 + C^2 = 1\) constrains the principal eigenvalue parameter \(p\) and the algebraic constant \(C\) of the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix. This constraint guarantees:

  • \(|\lambda_1| = 1\) (principal eigenvalue modulus 1, steady state exists)
  • \(|\lambda_{2,3}| < 1\) (non-principal eigenvalue moduli strictly less than 1, dissipation exists)

If algebraic normalization did not hold, one might encounter \(|\lambda_{2,3}| = 1\) (no dissipation) or \(|\lambda_{2,3}| > 1\) (unstable) — the former yields no arrow, the latter yields no steady state. Hence algebraic normalization is a necessary condition for the existence of the arrow of time.

3.4 Arrow and Born Rule

The Born rule \(\Delta\lambda_i \propto \sqrt{\sigma_i}\) relates coding weights to spectral gaps. The strength of the arrow of time is also associated with coding weights via the Born rule:

\[\sigma_S \propto \rho_{\mathcal{C}} \cdot (1 - |\lambda_2|^2) = 3\sigma_{\mathcal{C}} \cdot (1 - |\lambda_2|^2).\]

Thus the larger the coding weight of the Causal Sector (larger \(\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}\)), the stronger the arrow of time. At our position \(\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}^* \approx 0.210603\), the arrow strength is moderate.

Comparative Discussion: Born Rule and Fluctuation-Dissipation Theorem

In standard statistical mechanics, the fluctuation-dissipation theorem relates dissipation (transport coefficients) to fluctuations (correlation functions). In the causal field framework, the Born rule \(\Delta\lambda_i \propto \sqrt{\sigma_i}\) plays a similar role:

§5 Physical Consequences of the Arrow

5.1 Irreversibility of Causal Order

Monotonically increasing entropy implies that the evolution of the causal field is irreversible — past states cannot be recovered from the current state. This is the precise mathematical expression of causal order:

  • Past: Low-entropy state (ordered), information-rich
  • Future: High-entropy state (disordered), information-poor
  • Direction: From past to future, the direction of entropy increase

Comparative Discussion: Causal Order vs Thermodynamic Order

The distinction between standard thermodynamic order (Clausius: entropy increase principle) and causal order:

Property Thermodynamic Order Causal Order
Foundation Second law of thermodynamics Liouville lift + Birkhoff dissipation
Origin of irreversibility Statistical (molecular chaos) Algebraic (\(|\lambda_{2,3}|<1\))
Scope of applicability Macroscopic systems Causal field (any scale)
Initial condition Requires low-entropy initial state Not required (algebraic necessity)
Reversibility Approximately reversible statistically Algebraically strictly irreversible

Causal order is a more fundamental irreversibility than thermodynamic order — it is embedded in the algebraic structure and does not depend on statistical approximation or initial conditions.

5.2 Balance of Memory and Forgetting

The entropy increase of the causal field (information production) and the attenuation of the information field (information forgetting) form a balance:

\[\underbrace{\frac{dS_{\mathcal{C}}}{dt}}_{\geq 0 \text{ (causal field produces information)}} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \underbrace{\Gamma_{\text{geo}} \cdot \rho_{\mathcal{I}}}_{\geq 0 \text{ (information field forgets information)}}.\]

The causal field continuously produces new information, and the information field continuously forgets old information — the two balance each other, maintaining the system in steady state. This balance is the physical foundation of the Information Field Attenuation Theorem of Vol. 5.

5.3 Causal Arrow and Observer

The observer, as a subsystem of the information field, perceives time via the causal arrow:

  • The observer’s “past” is the memory of low-entropy states
  • The observer’s “future” is the expectation of high-entropy states
  • The observer’s “present” is the current deviation from steady state

The causal arrow not only defines the direction of physical time but also defines the observer’s subjective experience of time.

Comparative Discussion: Causal Arrow vs Psychological Arrow of Time

In standard physics, the psychological arrow of time (our subjective perception of the passage of time) is usually linked to the thermodynamic arrow. The causal field framework provides a more precise connection:

The observer (information field subsystem) perceives the entropy increase of the causal field through the \(\mathcal{C}\)-\(\mathcal{I}\) coupling (\(\theta_{\sigma_2} \approx 0.009\)), forming a subjective experience of time.
