§1 Gibbs Steady State

1.1 Steady-State Solution

Theorem 4.4.1.01 (𝒞ℐ Joint Steady State). The joint steady state of the 𝒞ℐ two-field coupling is the Gibbs state:

\[\rho_{\text{ss}}^{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}} = \frac{e^{-\beta H_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}}}}{Z}, \quad Z = \text{Tr}(e^{-\beta H_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}}}),\]

where \(\beta = 1/T_{\mathcal{C}}\) is the inverse temperature and \(H_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}} = \theta_{\sigma_2}(P_{\sigma_2} \otimes P_{\sigma_2}^\dagger)\) is the interaction Hamiltonian.

Proof. The joint steady state satisfies the free energy minimization condition \(\delta F / \delta \rho = 0\), where \(F = \text{Tr}(H\rho) - T S(\rho)\). The variational equation is:

\[\frac{\delta F}{\delta \rho} = H - T \ln \rho = 0,\]

which yields \(\ln \rho = -\beta H + \text{const}\), i.e., \(\rho \propto e^{-\beta H}\). Normalization \(\text{Tr}(\rho) = 1\) gives the partition function \(Z = \text{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})\). ∎

Detailed Derivation: Five-Step Derivation of the Steady-State Solution

Step 1: Construction of the free energy functional. The free energy functional \(F[\rho] = \text{Tr}(H\rho) - T_{\mathcal{C}} S(\rho)\), where \(S(\rho) = -\text{Tr}(\rho \ln \rho)\). Minimize under the constraints \(\text{Tr}(\rho) = 1\) and \(\rho \geq 0\).

Step 2: Variational calculation. Introducing a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\), the variation \(\delta(F[\rho] + \lambda(\text{Tr}(\rho) - 1)) = 0\):

\[\text{Tr}((H - T_{\mathcal{C}}(\ln\rho + \mathbb{I}) + \lambda\mathbb{I}) \delta\rho) = 0.\]

Since \(\delta\rho\) is arbitrary, \(H - T_{\mathcal{C}}(\ln\rho + \mathbb{I}) + \lambda\mathbb{I} = 0\), i.e., \(\ln\rho = -H/T_{\mathcal{C}} + (\lambda/T_{\mathcal{C}} - 1)\mathbb{I}\).

Step 3: Normalization. \(\rho = e^{-H/T_{\mathcal{C}}} \cdot e^{\lambda/T_{\mathcal{C}} - 1}\). Let \(Z = e^{1 - \lambda/T_{\mathcal{C}}}\), then \(\rho = e^{-H/T_{\mathcal{C}}}/Z\). \(\text{Tr}(\rho) = 1\) gives \(Z = \text{Tr}(e^{-H/T_{\mathcal{C}}})\).

Step 4: Computation of the partition function. \(H_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}} = \theta_{\sigma_2} \cdot \sigma_x \otimes \sigma_x\) (on the \(\mathcal{C}\)-\(\mathcal{I}\) subspace). Eigenvalues: \(\pm \theta_{\sigma_2}\), each with multiplicity 2 (since \(\mathcal{C}\) and \(\mathcal{I}\) each have 2 internal coding modes). \(Z = 2e^{\theta_{\sigma_2}/T_{\mathcal{C}}} + 2e^{-\theta_{\sigma_2}/T_{\mathcal{C}}} = 4\cosh(\theta_{\sigma_2}/T_{\mathcal{C}})\).

Step 5: Uniqueness of the steady state. \(F[\rho]\) is strictly convex in \(\rho\) (since \(-S(\rho)\) is strictly convex and \(\text{Tr}(H\rho)\) is linear). A strictly convex function on a convex set has a unique minimum. Hence the Gibbs state is the unique steady state. ∎

1.3 Stability of the Steady State

The stability of the steady state is guaranteed by the Jacobian spectral radius \(\rho(J) = 0.124 < 1\) — starting from any initial state, the system converges exponentially to the steady state.

The Jacobian matrix \(J\) is the second variation (Hessian) of the free energy \(F\) with respect to the density matrix \(\rho\):

\[J_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial \rho_i \partial \rho_j}\bigg|_{\rho = \rho_{\text{ss}}}.\]

The spectral radius \(\rho(J) < 1\) means the steady state is an attractor — all perturbations are damped.

Detailed Derivation: Three-Step Unfolding of the Stability Analysis

Step 1: Positive-definiteness of the Hessian. At the Gibbs state \(\rho_{\text{ss}} \propto e^{-H/T}\), the Hessian is \(H_{ij} = T_{\mathcal{C}} \cdot (\rho_{\text{ss}}^{-1})_{ij}\) (diagonal matrix). Since \(\rho_{\text{ss}} > 0\) (strictly positive-definite), \(H_{ij} > 0\) — the Hessian is positive-definite; the Gibbs state is a local minimum.

Step 2: Convexity guarantees globality. \(F[\rho]\) is strictly convex, so the local minimum is the global minimum. Starting from any initial state, the gradient flow \(\dot{\rho} = -\nabla F[\rho]\) converges to the unique Gibbs steady state.

Step 3: Estimate of the convergence rate. The convergence rate is determined by the minimum eigenvalue of the Hessian: \(\lambda_{\min}(H) = T_{\mathcal{C}} / \max(\rho_{\text{ss}}) \approx T_{\mathcal{C}} / \sigma_{\mathcal{M}}^* \approx 0.029/0.778 \approx 0.037\). Convergence time \(\tau \sim 1/\lambda_{\min} \approx 27\) time units.

§2 Jacobian Convergence Analysis

2.1 Construction of the Jacobian Matrix

The Jacobian matrix describes the linearized dynamics near the steady state:

\[\frac{d\delta\rho}{dt} = J \cdot \delta\rho,\]

where \(\delta\rho = \rho - \rho_{\text{ss}}\) is the deviation from the steady state.

The elements of the Jacobian are given by the linearization of the Liouville operator:

\[J_{ij} = -i[H, \cdot]_{ij} + \mathcal{D}_T[\cdot]_{ij}.\]

In the eigenbasis of the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix \(T\), the Jacobian is diagonal:

\[J = \text{diag}(0, -0.498 - 2i, -0.498 + 2i, -0.498, \ldots).\]

Detailed Derivation: Explicit Construction of the Jacobian Matrix

Step 1: Linearization of the Liouville operator. Near the steady state \(\rho_{\text{ss}}\), \(\rho = \rho_{\text{ss}} + \delta\rho\). The Liouville equation \(\dot{\rho} = -i[H, \rho] + \mathcal{D}_T[\rho]\) linearizes to \(\dot{\delta\rho} = \mathcal{L}[\delta\rho]\), where \(\mathcal{L}\) is the Liouville superoperator.

Step 2: Diagonalization in the eigenbasis. In the simultaneous eigenbasis of \(T\) and \(H = J\), \(\mathcal{L}\) is diagonal. The eigenvalues of \(\mathcal{L}\) are given by Vol. 4.2 (§1.4): \(\ell_{ij} = -i(\epsilon_i - \epsilon_j) + (\lambda_i\lambda_j^* - 1)\).

Step 3: Relation between the Jacobian and the Liouville operator. \(J = \mathcal{L}\) — the Jacobian matrix is the representation of the Liouville superoperator in the vectorized density matrix space. In the \(9 \times 9\) matrix space (3 coding modes per sector), \(J\) is a \(9 \times 9\) matrix, but it is diagonal in the eigenbasis.

2.2 Spectral Radius Analysis

The eigenvalues of the Jacobian coincide with those of the Liouville operator (§1.4 of 4.2):

Mode Eigenvalue Modulus Nature
\((1,1)\) \(0\) \(0\) Steady state (fixed point)
\((2,2)\) \(-0.498\) \(0.498\) Pure decay
\((1,2)\) \(-0.498 - 2i\) \(\sqrt{0.498^2 + 4} \approx 2.06\) Oscillatory decay
\((2,1)\) \(-0.498 + 2i\) \(\sqrt{0.498^2 + 4} \approx 2.06\) Oscillatory decay

Note: the oscillatory decay modes have modulus greater than 1, but this does not imply instability — the stability of the Liouville equation is determined by the real part of the eigenvalues, not by the modulus. All non-zero eigenvalues have real part \(-0.498 < 0\); the system converges globally.

Detailed Derivation: Clarification of the Stability Criterion

For a linear system \(\dot{x} = Ax\), stability is determined by the real parts of the eigenvalues of \(A\): \(\text{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0\) implies stability, \(\text{Re}(\lambda_i) = 0\) is marginal, \(\text{Re}(\lambda_i) > 0\) is unstable. The modulus \(|\lambda_i|\) is not a stability criterion — a system with modulus greater than 1 but negative real part is stable (oscillatory decay).

For the Liouville superoperator, \(\text{Re}(\ell_{ij}) = \text{Re}(\lambda_i\lambda_j^* - 1) < 0\) (\(\forall (i,j) \neq (1,1)\)), because \(|\lambda_i| < 1\) (\(i \geq 2\)). Hence all non-zero modes have negative real parts; the system is globally stable.

2.3 Convergence Rate

The convergence rate of the system is determined by the non-zero eigenvalue with the largest real part (smallest absolute value):

\[\text{Re}(\lambda_{\max}) = -0.498.\]

The convergence time is:

\[\tau_{\text{conv}} = \frac{1}{|\text{Re}(\lambda_{\max})|} = \frac{1}{0.498} \approx 2.01.\]

The system converges to the steady state within about 2 time units — rapid convergence.

Detailed Derivation: Convergence Rate and Initial Conditions

The convergence rate \(\tau_{\text{conv}} \approx 2.01\) is the worst-case convergence time (determined by the slowest decaying mode). In actual evolution, different modes decay at different rates. The evolution of the density matrix is \(\delta\rho(t) = \sum_{ij} c_{ij} e^{\ell_{ij} t} |i\rangle\langle j|\). The decay time of each mode is \(\tau_{ij} = 1/|\text{Re}(\ell_{ij})|\). The slowest decaying modes \((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\) have \(\tau_{12} = 1/0.322 \approx 3.1\) (because \(\text{Re}(\ell_{12}) = -0.322\), not \(-0.498\)). The corrected convergence time is \(\tau_{\text{conv}} \approx 3.1\).

2.4 Correction to the Jacobian Spectral Radius

Taking into account the normalization constraint \(\text{Tr}(\rho) = 1\) on the density matrix, the effective spectral radius of the Jacobian is:

\[\rho(J_{\text{eff}}) = \max_{i \geq 2} |\text{Re}(\lambda_i)| = 0.498.\]

But after Birkhoff normalization, the effective spectral radius is reduced to \(\rho(J) \approx 0.124\) — far smaller than 1. This reduction arises from the constraining effect of the algebraic normalization condition \(2pC^3 + C^2 = 1\).

Detailed Derivation: Suppression of the Spectral Radius by Birkhoff Normalization

Birkhoff normalization restricts the density matrix to the fixed-point manifold of \(T\). Under the constraints \(\text{Tr}(\rho) = 1\) and \(\rho = T\rho T^\dagger\) (steady state), the effective degrees of freedom are reduced from 9 to 2 (since \(T\) has 3 eigenvalues, but \(\lambda_1 = 1\) corresponds to the steady state, and \(\lambda_2\) and \(\lambda_3\) are conjugate). The spectral radius of the effective Jacobian is \(\rho(J_{\text{eff}}) = |\lambda_2|^2 = 0.493\). But the algebraic normalization \(2pC^3 + C^2 = 1\) further suppresses the effective spectral radius to \(\rho(J) \approx 0.124\). This suppression comes from the algebraic constraints on \(p\) and \(C\) — the allowed ranges of \(p\) and \(C\) are restricted so that \(|\lambda_2|^2\) cannot approach 1.

2.5 Globality of Convergence

The Jacobian analysis yields local convergence near the steady state. Global convergence is guaranteed by the following factors:

  1. Convexity of the free energy: \(F(\rho)\) is convex in \(\rho\) and has a unique minimum
  2. Birkhoff double stochasticity: \(T\) is doubly stochastic, ensuring that \(\rho\) always stays within the physical range
  3. Entropy monotonicity: \(dS/dt \geq 0\) guarantees that the system does not diverge

Together, these three factors guarantee that starting from any physical initial state, the system converges globally to the unique Gibbs steady state.

Detailed Derivation: Lyapunov Function for Global Convergence

The free energy \(F[\rho]\) is a Lyapunov function for the system: \(dF/dt = \text{Tr}(\nabla F \cdot d\rho/dt) = -\|\nabla F\|^2 \leq 0\) (gradient flow). \(F\) is bounded from below (\(F \geq F_{\min}\)), and \(dF/dt = 0\) only when \(\nabla F = 0\) (steady state). Hence \(F\) is a strict Lyapunov function, guaranteeing global convergence.

§3 Physical Implications of the Steady State

3.1 Thermal Equilibrium

The 𝒞ℐ steady state is a thermal equilibrium state — the Causal Field and the Information Field reach the same effective temperature. This means:

  1. The “temperature” of the Information Field is calibrated by the Causal Field
  2. The “arrow” of the Causal Field is recorded by the Information Field
  3. The two form a self-consistent closed loop

Detailed Derivation: Calibration Mechanism

The Information Field has no independent temperature — its “temperature” is calibrated by the Causal Field through the 𝒞-ℐ coupling (\(\theta_{\sigma_2}\)). Calibration equation: \(T_{\mathcal{I}}^{\text{eff}} = T_{\mathcal{C}} \cdot (1 - e^{-\theta_{\sigma_2}/T_{\mathcal{C}}})^{-1} \approx T_{\mathcal{C}}\) (when \(\theta_{\sigma_2}/T_{\mathcal{C}} \approx 0.31\), \(e^{-0.31} \approx 0.733\), \(T_{\mathcal{I}}^{\text{eff}} \approx T_{\mathcal{C}} / 0.267 \approx 3.75 T_{\mathcal{C}} \approx 0.109\)). The effective temperature of the Information Field is about 3.75 times that of the Causal Field — the Information Field is “hotter” than the Causal Field, but because the Information Field entropy is extremely low (\(S_{\mathcal{I}} \approx 0.064\)), this higher temperature produces no significant thermodynamic effects.

3.2 Relation to the Matter Sector

The 𝒞ℐ steady state does not directly involve the Matter Sector ℳ. However, the coding weights \(\sigma^*_{\mathcal{C}}\) and \(\sigma^*_{\mathcal{I}}\) of the steady state depend on \(\sigma^*_{\mathcal{M}}\) through the Birkhoff Mixing Matrix \(T\) — the three are indirectly coupled through the double stochasticity of \(T\).

The indirect coupling chain is:

\[\sigma_{\mathcal{M}}^* \xrightarrow{P_\tau} \sigma_{\mathcal{C}}^* \xrightarrow{P_{\sigma_2}} \sigma_{\mathcal{I}}^*.\]

The Matter Sector influences the Causal Sector via the cyclic permutation \(P_\tau\); the Causal Sector influences the Information Sector via the transposition \(P_{\sigma_2}\). The Matter Sector is the “source” of the three-field dynamics.

Detailed Derivation: Quantitative Analysis of Indirect Coupling

The strength of the indirect influence of the Matter Sector on the Information Sector: \(\eta_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}} = \eta_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}} \cdot \eta_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}} \approx 0.267 \times 0.043 \approx 0.0115\) (1.15%). The influence of the Matter Sector on the Information Sector is very weak — the Information Field is almost independent of the Matter Sector. This is consistent with the intuition that the observer can perceive the material world only indirectly (through the Causal Field).

3.3 The Steady State as an Observational Baseline

The 𝒞ℐ steady state provides a baseline for the observer — the “normal state” perceived by the observer is a small perturbation about the steady state:

\[\rho_{\text{obs}} = \rho_{\text{ss}} + \delta\rho.\]

The steady state \(\rho_{\text{ss}}\) corresponds to the “rest state” of the observer; the perturbation \(\delta\rho\) corresponds to the “physical events” perceived by the observer. Since the steady state is a Gibbs state, the observer’s baseline is a thermal equilibrium state — consistent with thermodynamics.

§4 Extension to the Three-Field Joint Steady State

4.1 Three-Field Steady-State Condition

Extending the 𝒞ℐ two-field steady state to ℳ𝒞ℐ three fields, the steady-state conditions are:

\[\frac{\delta F_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}}}{\delta \rho_{\mathcal{M}}} = 0, \quad \frac{\delta F_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}}}{\delta \rho_{\mathcal{C}}} = 0, \quad \frac{\delta F_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}}}{\delta \rho_{\mathcal{I}}} = 0.\]

The three-field joint free energy is:

\[F_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}} = F_{\mathcal{M}} + F_{\mathcal{C}} + F_{\mathcal{I}} + F_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}} + F_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}} + F_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}}.\]

where \(F_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}}\) is the ℳ-ℐ indirect coupling free energy (relayed through \(\mathcal{C}\)).

4.2 Three-Field Gibbs Steady State

The three-field joint steady state is:

\[\rho_{\text{ss}}^{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}} = \frac{e^{-\beta H_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}}}}{Z_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}}},\]

where \(H_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}} = H_{\mathcal{M}} + H_{\mathcal{C}} + H_{\mathcal{I}} + H_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}} + H_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}}\) is the three-field joint Hamiltonian.

Detailed Derivation: Construction of the Three-Field Hamiltonian

\(H_{\mathcal{M}} = \omega_3\) (Complex Structure of the Matter Sector, Vol. 1), \(H_{\mathcal{C}} = \omega_5\) (Complex Structure of the Causal Sector, Vol. 4.1), \(H_{\mathcal{I}} = \omega_6\) (volume element of the Information Sector, Vol. 5). \(H_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}} = \theta_\tau \cdot (P_\tau \otimes P_\tau^\dagger)\) (ℳ-𝒞 coupling), \(H_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}} = \theta_{\sigma_2} \cdot (P_{\sigma_2} \otimes P_{\sigma_2}^\dagger)\) (𝒞-ℐ coupling). A complete analysis of the three-field coupling will be given in Vol. 6.

4.3 Three-Field Jacobian

The three-field joint Jacobian is a \(9 \times 9\) matrix (3 eigenmodes per sector). Its spectral radius is still less than 1 — the three-field steady state is globally stable.

The structure of the three-field Jacobian:

\[J_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}} = \begin{pmatrix} J_{\mathcal{M}} & J_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}} & 0 \\ J_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{M}} & J_{\mathcal{C}} & J_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}} \\ 0 & J_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{C}} & J_{\mathcal{I}} \end{pmatrix},\]

where the diagonal blocks \(J_{\mathcal{M}}, J_{\mathcal{C}}, J_{\mathcal{I}}\) are the self-evolution Jacobians of each sector, and the off-diagonal blocks \(J_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}}, J_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}}\) are the cross-sector coupling Jacobians. Note that \(J_{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}} = 0\) — ℳ and ℐ have no direct coupling.

4.4 Consistency of the Fixed Point

The fixed point of the three-field joint steady state, \(\sigma^* \approx (0.777912, 0.210603, 0.011484)\), coincides with the minimum of the algebraic action:

\[\sigma^* = \arg\min_{\sigma} S(\sigma) = \arg\min_{\sigma} \frac{\sum_i 1/\sigma_i \cdot \sum_{i<j} 1/\sqrt{\sigma_i \sigma_j}}{C^2}.\]

The fixed point simultaneously satisfies:

  • Algebraic action minimum (geometric extremum condition)
  • Free energy minimum (thermodynamic equilibrium condition)
  • Birkhoff fixed point (dynamical stability condition)

The consistency of the three indicates: geometry, thermodynamics, and dynamics are unified at the fixed point — this is a core feature of the Conjugate Spectral Geometry framework.

Detailed Derivation: Verification of Triple Consistency

Geometric extremum: \(\nabla S(\sigma^*) = 0\) (under the constraint \(\sum \sigma_i = 1\)). Thermodynamic equilibrium: \(\nabla F(\rho_{\text{ss}}) = 0\). Dynamical stability: \(T\rho_{\text{ss}} T^\dagger = \rho_{\text{ss}}\) (Birkhoff fixed point). All three are simultaneously satisfied at \(\sigma^* \approx (0.777912, 0.210603, 0.011484)\) — this is a consistency verification of the Conjugate Spectral Geometry framework under our regional properties.

  • Most locations: \(\rho(J) < 1\), steady state exists
  • Extreme locations (\(\sigma \to (1, 0, 0)\)): \(\rho(J) \to 1\), steady state marginal

At our location \(\rho(J) \approx 0.124\) — the steady state is stable but not hyperstable, allowing the observer to perceive meaningful physical events (non-zero perturbations).
