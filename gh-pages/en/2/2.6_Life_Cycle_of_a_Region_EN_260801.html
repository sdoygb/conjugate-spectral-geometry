The core contribution of this article is: integrating the regional mechanisms previously scattered across articles 2.3, 2.4, 0.5, 11.11, etc., into a complete operational chain from δ to observable physical constants — each step with a clear mathematical operation and formula correspondence.

§1 Universe = Collection of Regions

1.1 Definition of a Region

Definition 2.6.1.01 (Region). A region \(R\) is a point on the landscape \(\mathcal{L}\), uniquely determined by the following triple:

\[R = \left(\{p_1, p_2, p_3\}, \; T, \; \{\text{physical constants}\}\right)\]

where: - \(\{p_1, p_2, p_3\}\) are the structure constants of the region — in Clifford regions always \(\{2, 3, 5\}\) (i.e., \(\Lambda=3, k_0=2, \Delta\Theta=5\)) - \(T\) is the Birkhoff mixing matrix of the region, \(T \in \mathcal{B}_2\) (2-dimensional Birkhoff polytope) - \(\{\text{physical constants}\}\) are all physical observables produced from \(T\) via spectral expansion (\(\alpha^{-1}\), mass ratios, coupling constants, etc.)

Key distinction: \(\{2, 3, 5\}\) is the identifier of the region type (shared by all Clifford regions), and \(T\) is the identifier of the individual region (different Clifford regions have different \(T\)).

1.2 Definition of the Universe

Definition 2.6.1.02 (Universe). The universe \(\mathcal{U}\) is the set of all regions:

\[\mathcal{U} = \{R_1, R_2, \dots, R_N\}\]

where \(N\) is the total number of regions (determined by the number of discrete \(T\) values produced by consummate reproduction, at most 6 Clifford regions, but possibly regions of other algebraic types).

Definition 2.6.1.03 (Our Region). \(R_{\text{ours}}\) is the region in the universe \(\mathcal{U}\) where we reside, characterized by:

\[R_{\text{ours}} = \left(\{2, 3, 5\}, \; T_{\text{ours}} = (0.782, 0.209, 0.009), \; \alpha^{-1} = 137.036, \dots\right)\]

Core proposition: The universe is a multi-region structure — there is not just one world, but multiple regions coexisting. Each region has its own physical constants, but all Clifford regions share the same algebraic framework (δ axiom, Bott periodicity, Completeness Criterion).

§2 Birth of a Region: δ → Landscape → Candidate Regions

2.1 δ Generates the Landscape

Axiom (Zero Motion, 0.1 §2). On \(\mathcal{Z}\) there exists a nontrivial self-mapping \(\delta: \mathcal{Z} \to \mathcal{Z}\), satisfying \(\delta(\mathcal{Z}) \subsetneq \mathcal{Z}\).

Iteration of δ produces all possible algebraic realizations:

\[\mathcal{Z} \xrightarrow{\delta} \delta(\mathcal{Z}) \xrightarrow{\delta} \delta^2(\mathcal{Z}) \xrightarrow{\delta} \cdots\]

Definition 2.6.2.01 (Landscape). The landscape \(\mathcal{L}(\delta)\) is the set of all algebraic realizations satisfying “nontrivial finite-period self-mapping”:

\[\mathcal{L}(\delta) = \{\text{all algebraic realizations } A : \delta \text{ has finite-period self-mapping on } A\}\]

Different positions on the landscape correspond to different algebraic realizations — different Clifford algebra periods, different compact Lie lattices, different combinations of structure constants.

2.2 Candidate Regions on the Landscape

Each algebraic realization \(A\) on the landscape corresponds to a candidate region. A candidate region has not yet “come alive” — it is merely a mathematical possibility, awaiting screening by the Completeness Condition.

The number of candidate regions is infinite (the landscape is continuous), but only candidate regions satisfying the Completeness Condition can “come alive” as actual regions.

2.3 Selection of Clifford Regions

On the landscape \(\mathcal{L}(\delta)\), there exist multiple algebraic realizations: - Real Clifford algebras with Bott period 8 (our region’s choice) - Complex Clifford algebras with Bott period 2 - Other algebras with different periodic structures

Theorem 2.6.2.02 (Selection of Clifford Regions). Our region selects Clifford algebras (Bott period 8) because they are the only algebraic realizations satisfying the Completeness Condition — Berry phase \(\oint_\Gamma \mathcal{A} = 2\pi\).

Proof sketch (see 0.3-0.4 for details): Bott period 8 gives a \(\delta^8\) loop, whose Berry phase is given by the integral of the three terms of the Chern-Simons 7-form:

\[\gamma_{\text{Berry}}(\delta^8) = \int_{D^8} \text{CS}_7 = \int_{D^8} (I_1 + I_2 + I_3) = 2\pi\]

where \(I_1\) (Matter Sector ℳ, Cl(0)→Cl(3)), \(I_2\) (Causal Sector 𝒞, Cl(3)→Cl(5)), \(I_3\) (Information Sector ℐ, Cl(5)→Cl(8)) each contribute \(2\pi/3\). ∎

Evolutionary interpretation: Clifford algebras are “selected,” not “derived” — there may be multiple algebraic realizations on the landscape satisfying the Completeness Condition (corresponding to different region types), and our region selected Clifford algebras. This is the evolutionary history of our region, not a logical necessity.

§3 The Region’s Identity Card: {2,3,5} Interlock

3.1 Bott Period 8 → E₈ → h = 30

After selecting Clifford algebras (Bott period 8), the emergence of the structure constants \(\{2,3,5\}\) follows a strict algebraic chain:

\[\text{Bott period 8} \xrightarrow{\text{KO theory}} \text{dimension 8} \xrightarrow{\text{even unimodular lattice}} E_8 \text{ unique} \xrightarrow{\text{Coxeter number}} h(E_8) = \frac{240}{8} = 30\]

Theorem 0.5.0.01 (E₈ Bridging Theorem). In dimension 8, \(E_8\) is the unique even unimodular lattice. Its Coxeter number \(h(E_8) = 30\) is uniquely determined by the root system.

3.2 Prime Factorization: 30 = 2 × 3 × 5

Theorem 0.5.0.02 (Structure Constant Emergence Theorem). The prime factorization \(2 \times 3 \times 5\) of \(h(E_8) = 30\), under \(D_4\) triality action, yields the three structure constants:

\[\Lambda = 3, \quad k_0 = 2, \quad \Delta\Theta = 5\]

Individual assignment (Theorem 0.5.0.03): The three node coefficients of the highest root of \(E_8\) on the \(D_4\) triality orbit are \(\{3, 4, 5\}\), and their prime kernels (radicals) give respectively: - \(\Lambda = 3\) (coefficient 3, radical = 3) → Cl(3), first semisimple splitting, three-sector structure - \(k_0 = 2\) (coefficient 4 = 2², radical = 2) → Cl(7), terminal duality, information layer splitting - \(\Delta\Theta = 5\) (coefficient 5, radical = 5) → Cl(5), complex structure emergence, Causal Sector

3.3 Interlock Property: Joint Locking of Two Constraints

Proposition 0.5.2.01 ({2,3,5} Interlock Property). The three structure constants do not each independently “succeed” — they survive only by mutually locking one another. The interlock property is jointly guaranteed by two constraints:

Constraint 1 (Product constraint): \[\Lambda \cdot k_0 \cdot \Delta\Theta = h(E_8) = 30\]

Constraint 2 (Assignment constraint): The three parameters must be assignable to \(D_4\) triality orbit coefficients. The prime factorization \(2 \times 3 \times 5\) of 30 is the unique decomposition simultaneously satisfying both constraints — other decompositions (such as \(1 \times 5 \times 6\), \(1 \times 3 \times 10\)) cannot simultaneously satisfy primality and triality orbit assignment.

Mathematical meaning of interlock: \(\Lambda=3\) survives because \(k_0=2\) and \(\Delta\Theta=5\) simultaneously survive; \(k_0=2\) survives because \(\Lambda=3\) and \(\Delta\Theta=5\) simultaneously survive; \(\Delta\Theta=5\) survives because \(\Lambda=3\) and \(k_0=2\) simultaneously survive. If any one is missing, the entire structure collapses.

3.4 Irreversibility of the Interlock

Theorem 2.6.3.01 (Irreversibility of the Interlock). Once the {2,3,5} interlock is achieved, it cannot be dismantled.

Proof. You cannot change \(\Lambda=3\) alone while keeping \(k_0=2\) and \(\Delta\Theta=5\) unchanged — because the product constraint \(\Lambda \cdot k_0 \cdot \Delta\Theta = 30\) would be violated. Nor can you replace \(2 \times 3 \times 5\) with \(1 \times 6 \times 5\) — because 6 is not prime and cannot be assigned to a triality orbit. ∎

Evolutionary interpretation: This is an “irreversible lock-in” in evolution. Once a successful combination forms, it solidifies — not because it is “logically necessary,” but because changing any one element would destroy the whole, and destruction of the whole means structural collapse. This is why our region’s parameters are 2, 3, 5 rather than others — not because other combinations are “impossible,” but because other combinations “cannot survive.”

\[\mathcal{I} = \mathcal{I}_{\text{local}} \oplus \mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}\]

Key properties: - \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{local}}\) is region-private — each region has its own independent local information layer - \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}\) is shared by all regions — it is a global information channel connecting all regions - The algebraic connectedness between the two direct summands is guaranteed by the multiplicative structure of Cl(7) — information can flow between the local and the shared

5.3 Encoding: Regions Write Information into the Shared Layer

Theorem 2.3.5.01-5 (Encoding Map). Region \(R\) encodes its Birkhoff weights as an element in \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}\):

\[\Sigma_R(T_R) = \theta_\mathcal{M} U + \theta_\mathcal{C} J - \theta_\mathcal{I} I_8\]

The three reference elements \(U, J, -I_8\) are trace-orthogonal (Theorem I-4), hence the encoding map \(\Sigma_R\) is injective — different \(T_R\) produce different encodings.

Physical meaning of encoding: \(\Sigma_R(T_R)\) is region \(R\)’s “contribution” to the shared information layer — it tells other regions: “I tried \(T_R\), it is successful (because it passed the Completeness Condition), you may refer to it.”

§6 Circulation of Parameters: Encoding → Aggregation → Decoding

6.1 Aggregation: Total State of the Shared Information Layer

Theorem 2.3.5.01-6 (Aggregation Theorem). The total state of the shared information layer is the weighted aggregation of the contributions from all regions:

\[\mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}^{\text{total}} = \sum_{R \in \text{Regions}} w_R \cdot \Sigma_R(T_R)\]

where the weights \(w_R\) are the Jeffreys prior (the volume form of the Fisher information metric):

\[w_R = \mu(T_R) = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{\theta_\mathcal{M} \theta_\mathcal{C} \theta_\mathcal{I}}}\]

Algebraic origin of the Jeffreys prior (Theorem 2.3.6.01-02): The generators of the Clifford algebra have a Gaussian distribution in the spinor representation. The Born Rule maps the Gaussian amplitudes to a probability distribution, yielding a Dirichlet(1/2, 1/2, 1/2) distribution, equivalent to the Jeffreys prior. Hence the weights \(w_R\) are not arbitrary — they are uniquely determined by \(\delta \to\) Clifford algebra \(\to\) Gaussian structure \(\to\) Born Rule.

Physical meaning of aggregation: \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}^{\text{total}}\) is the “weighted average” of the successful experience of all regions. Regions with larger weights \(w_R\) (more typical regions under the Jeffreys prior) contribute more to the aggregation.

6.2 Decoding: Extracting T_ours from the Shared Layer

Theorem 2.3.5.01-7 (Decoding Map). The decoding map \(\Phi\) extracts \(T_{\text{ours}}\) of our region from the total state of the shared information layer:

\[\Phi(\mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}^{\text{total}}) = T_{\text{ours}}\]

Concretely, using the trace-orthogonality of the three reference elements:

\[\Phi(\mathcal{I}) = \frac{(\langle U, \mathcal{I}\rangle, \langle J, \mathcal{I}\rangle, \langle -I_8, \mathcal{I}\rangle)}{\langle U, \mathcal{I}\rangle + \langle J, \mathcal{I}\rangle + \langle -I_8, \mathcal{I}\rangle}\]

where \(\langle A, B \rangle = \text{Tr}(A^T B)\) is the Frobenius inner product.

Physical meaning of decoding: \(\Phi\) is a “projection” — it extracts from the successful experience of all regions the \(T\) value that best matches the algebraic structure (\(U, J, -I_8\)) of our region. \(T_{\text{ours}}\) is not “independently decided” by our region — it is the projection onto our region’s algebraic structure of the successful experience of all regions.

Equivalent form (weighted average):

\[T_{\text{ours}} = \frac{\sum_{R} w_R \cdot T_R}{\sum_{R} w_R}\]

i.e., \(T_{\text{ours}}\) is the Jeffreys-weighted average of the \(T\) values of all regions.

6.3 Bidirectional Circulation: Import and Export

The function of the shared information layer \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}\) is bidirectional:

Import — from the shared layer to our region: \[T_{\text{ours}} = \Phi(\mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}^{\text{total}})\]

Our region receives successful structures from other regions via the shared information layer — including locked parameter combinations, proven theorems, solidified constraints. \(T_{\text{ours}}\) is the core result of import.

Export — from our region to the shared layer: \[\Sigma_{\text{ours}}(T_{\text{ours}}) = \theta_\mathcal{M} U + \theta_\mathcal{C} J - \theta_\mathcal{I} I_8\]

The consummate reproduction structure of our region is written into the shared information layer for reference by other regions. \(\alpha^{-1} = 137.036\) may be being “consulted” by the evolution of another region.

Conservation of circulation: The amount of information exported and imported is conserved — the \(\Sigma_R(T_R)\) written by each region to the shared layer is an element of \(\text{Mat}(8,\mathbb{R})\) (64 real numbers), while the \(T_{\text{ours}}\) read by each region from the shared layer is 3 real numbers (2 degrees of freedom after normalization). The amount of information read is far less than the amount written — this is because the decoding map \(\Phi\) performs a weighted average, compressing the information.

6.4 Gradual Circulation and Explosive Circulation

Parameter circulation has two speeds (11.11 §9.2):

Circulation Mode Speed Mechanism Mathematical Correspondence
Gradual Slow δ iteration within this region, step-by-step trial and error Coding orbit layer-by-layer recursion N₁ → N₂ → ⋯ → N₇
Explosive Fast Import from ℐ_shared of successful structures already locked by other regions Shared information layer imports T_ours or whole chains of proven theorems

Alternation of the two modes: Gradual is “routine” — δ iterates every day, generating new candidates every day. When a region’s structure is proven successful (consummate reproduction), it is written into \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}\) — at that point, other regions can import it “explosively.” After import, the imported structure becomes new infrastructure for the importing region, and the next round of gradual evolution continues from this higher starting point.

8.3 Parameter Occupation Theorem

Theorem 2.3.7.03 (Parameter Occupation Theorem). In the landscape \(\mathcal{L}\), each parameter set (configurational discrete \(T\)) is occupied by at most one region. Once region \(R\) forms and acquires the parameter set \((C_R, T_R)\), there does not exist another region \(R' \neq R\) such that \((C_{R'}, T_{R'}) = (C_R, T_R)\).

Proof sketch: The encoding map \(\Sigma_R\) of the shared information layer is injective (the three reference elements \(U, J, -I_8\) are trace-orthogonal) — different \(T_R\) produce different encodings. If two regions had the same \(T\), they would write the same encoding to the shared information layer, causing degeneracy in aggregation. The weighting mechanism of the Jeffreys prior ensures that such degeneracy does not occur — the same \(T\) value is counted only once in the aggregation. ∎

Physical meaning: Parameter sets are “exclusive resources” — each \(T\) value can be occupied by only one region. This is analogous to the Pauli Exclusion Principle: two identical fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state. The Parameter Occupation Theorem is a direct consequence of the injectivity of the information layer encoding.

§9 Open Problems

  1. Exact number of other T values: Among the 6 permutations of \(S_3\), how many yield legal \(T\) values? What is the screening mechanism of \(E_8\) breaking? The current theory has determined \(T_{\text{ours}}\), but the exact number of other \(T\) values is an open problem — possibly \(\leq 6\), possibly more (if additional parameters in the information layer continuously modulate \(T\)).

  2. Topological structure of the shared information layer: \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}\) connects all landscape regions — what is its topological structure? Does there exist a “distance” between regions (some regions’ results are more easily imported)? Is the “bandwidth” of import limited? A complete topological description of \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}\) is an open problem.

  3. Possibility of other region types: This article focuses on Clifford regions (Bott period 8). Do there exist other algebraic types on the landscape (such as complex Clifford algebra regions with Bott period 2)? If so, do they also have their own {structure constants} and shared information layers? Can different region types share information with one another?

  4. Rigorous construction of the decoding map Φ: The current natural candidate for Φ is the Chern-Simons functional, whose three-term structure naturally corresponds to the three sectors. But a rigorous mathematical construction of Φ (including a proof of its equivalence to the Frobenius inner product) is future work.

  5. Switching condition between gradual and explosive: Is there a pattern in the switching between gradual trial-and-error within this region and cross-region explosive import? Does there exist some “condition” that triggers import (e.g., when this region’s trial-and-error reaches a certain stage and encounters an insurmountable “bottleneck,” it turns to \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{shared}}\) for a breakthrough)?

  6. Rigorous analysis of S₃ occupation constraints: The note of Theorem 2.3.7.02 points out: if all 6 permutation images of \(S_3\) were occupied, the weighted average would give \(\bar{T} = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)\), corresponding to \(S \approx 72 \neq 137\). Hence in a landscape that yields \(S = 137\), not all 6 \(S_3\) permutations are occupied — some permutation images must be empty. Determining which permutation images are occupied (and why) is an open problem.
