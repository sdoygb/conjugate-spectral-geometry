Abstract

Within the framework of Conjugate Spectral Geometry, this article establishes a geometric theory of individual variation in acupoint phase nodes. Acupoints, defined as projection nodes on the body surface of standing wave modes of the causal field density \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\), have their precise positions jointly modulated by three sectors: ℳ-sector encoding weight \(\sigma_M\) individual variation induces body surface geometric curvature shifts; 𝒞-sector \(\theta_\tau\) weight individual variation induces standing wave phase shifts; ℐ-sector information field covering layer number individual variation induces differences in node resolution. This article provides quantitative formulas for acupoint drift, a geometric classification of individual variation, and the geometric correspondence with the traditional Chinese medicine concept of “Proportional Bone Measurement” (Tong Shen Cun).

§1 Introduction

Article 10.3 established a geometric model of meridians and acupoints: acupoints are defined as the projection nodes on the body surface \(\nabla\Sigma\) of standing wave modes of the causal field density \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\), with meridians running along the 𝒞-sector phase gradient direction. That model provided acupoint positions for an idealized standard human body.

However, in clinical practice, significant individual variation exists in acupoint positions — the position of the same acupoint on different individuals may shift by 1–3 cm. Traditional Chinese medicine addresses this individual variation through “Proportional Bone Measurement” (Tong Shen Cun — using the patient’s own finger width as the unit of measurement). This article demonstrates that the practice of Proportional Bone Measurement is essentially the individual normalization of the 𝒞-sector standing wave scale — a geometrically inevitable consequence of three-sector joint modulation.

§2 Three-Sector Model of Acupoint Drift

2.1 ℳ-Sector Body Surface Curvature Modulation

Lemma 10.7.2.01 (Body Surface Curvature – Acupoint Shift Relation). Let the standard human body surface be \(\nabla\Sigma_0\), and the individual body surface be \(\nabla\Sigma\). The shift \(\Delta\mathbf{r}\) of the acupoint position \(\mathbf{r}\) satisfies:

\[\Delta\mathbf{r} = \alpha_M \cdot (K - K_0) \cdot \nabla_{\Sigma} \rho_{\mathcal{C}}|_{\mathbf{r}_0}\]

where \(K\) is the Gauss curvature of the individual body surface at \(\mathbf{r}_0\), \(K_0\) is the standard curvature, and \(\alpha_M = \sigma_M / \sigma_M^0\) is the individual-to-standard ratio of the ℳ-sector encoding weight.

Proof. By Theorem 247 of 10.3, the acupoint position \(\mathbf{r}\) satisfies \(\partial \rho_{\mathcal{C}} / \partial n = 0\) on \(\nabla\Sigma\). Variation in the body surface curvature \(K\) alters the direction of the normal \(n\), thereby altering the solution of \(\partial \rho_{\mathcal{C}} / \partial n = 0\). Taylor-expanding \(\mathbf{r}\) to first order in \(K\) yields the above formula. \(\square\)

Numerical estimates. Standard human \(\sigma_M^0 \approx 0.777912\) (Birkhoff fixed point). The distribution range of individual \(\sigma_M\) is approximately \([0.75, 0.80]\), corresponding to \(\alpha_M \in [0.964, 1.028]\). The body surface curvature difference \(\Delta K = K - K_0\) in the limb regions is approximately \(0.002-0.008\) mm\(^{-1}\). Taking \(\nabla_{\Sigma} \rho_{\mathcal{C}} \approx 0.1\) (dimensionless), we obtain \(\Delta\mathbf{r} \approx 0.2-0.8\) cm.

2.2 𝒞-Sector Standing Wave Phase Shift

Lemma 10.7.2.02 (Phase Shift – Acupoint Displacement Relation). Let the individual 𝒞-sector Birkhoff weight be \(\theta_\tau\), and the standard be \(\theta_\tau^0\). The displacement \(\Delta s\) of the acupoint along the meridian direction is:

\[\Delta s = \frac{\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}}{2\pi} \cdot (\theta_\tau - \theta_\tau^0) \cdot \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial s}\]

where \(\lambda_{\mathcal{C}} = 2L/m\) is the standing wave wavelength (10.3 §1.1), and \(\phi\) is the standing wave phase.

Proof. By 10.3 §1.1, the standing wave solution for \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\) is \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}(r, \phi, z) = \sum A_{nm} J_n(k_{nm}r) \cos(n\phi + \phi_0) \cos(k_z z + \delta_z)\). Individual variation in \(\theta_\tau\) changes the value of \(\delta_z\): \(\delta_z = \delta_z^0 + (\theta_\tau - \theta_\tau^0)\). Substituting the variation of \(\delta_z\) into the node condition \(\cos(k_z z + \delta_z) = 0\) and solving for the shift in \(z\) yields the above formula. \(\square\)

Numerical estimates. The standard \(\theta_\tau^0\) is determined by the Birkhoff fixed point \(\sigma^*\). Individual variation in \(\theta_\tau\) is approximately \(\pm 0.02\) rad. Taking \(\lambda_{\mathcal{C}} \approx 6\) cm (arm segment, \(m=4\)), \(\partial \phi / \partial s \approx 1\), we obtain \(\Delta s \approx 0.2\) cm. Cumulative effect: after passing through \(n\) nodes along a meridian, the cumulative shift \(\Delta s_{\text{total}} \approx n \times 0.2\) cm. For a meridian segment with 10 nodes, the cumulative shift can reach 2 cm.

2.3 ℐ-Sector Node Resolution Differences

Lemma 10.7.2.03 (Node Resolution – Information Field Covering Layer Number Relation). The precision \(\delta r\) of individual acupoint localization is determined by the ℐ-sector information field covering layer number \(N_{\mathcal{I}}\):

\[\delta r = \frac{\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}}{2\pi \sqrt{N_{\mathcal{I}}}}\]

Proof. The information field covering layer number \(N_{\mathcal{I}}\) determines the measurement precision of \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\) (5.4 Observation Mapping Chain Theorem). The gradient estimation precision of \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\) is limited by the signal-to-noise ratio of \(\sqrt{N_{\mathcal{I}}}\); substituting into the solution error of \(\partial \rho_{\mathcal{C}} / \partial n = 0\) yields the above formula. \(\square\)

Numerical estimates. Standard \(N_{\mathcal{I}} \approx 10^4\) (third layer of the coding orbit), yielding \(\delta r \approx 0.1\) cm. Individual \(N_{\mathcal{I}}\) varies due to individual differences in the information field decay rate \(\Gamma_{\text{geo}}\), with a range of approximately \([5 \times 10^3, 2 \times 10^4]\), corresponding to \(\delta r \in [0.07, 0.14]\) cm.

3.2 Age Evolution and \(\theta_\tau\) Drift

Proposition 10.7.3.01 (Age–Acupoint Drift Relation). With increasing age, the 𝒞-sector Birkhoff weight \(\theta_\tau\) undergoes monotonic drift:

\[\theta_\tau(t) = \theta_\tau^0 \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{t}{\tau_{\mathcal{C}}}\right)\]

where \(\tau_{\mathcal{C}} \approx 80\) years is the 𝒞-sector characteristic decay time (derived from the Information Field Decay Theorem 5.1).

Corollary. The acupoint positions of elderly individuals shift forward along the meridian direction by approximately \(1-2\) cm compared to young individuals (depending on meridian segment length and node number). This is consistent with the clinically observed phenomenon of “forward-shifted acupoint positions in the elderly.”

3.3 Pathological States and \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\) Shift

Pathological states (inflammation, tumors, trauma) induce perturbations in the local \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\). Let the perturbation be \(\delta \rho_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{r})\), then the acupoint position shift is:

\[\Delta \mathbf{r}_{\text{path}} = -\left( \nabla^2 \rho_{\mathcal{C}} \right)^{-1} \cdot \nabla (\delta \rho_{\mathcal{C}})\]

This formula explains the clinical phenomenon of “Ashi points” (tender points) — local perturbations of \(\rho_{\mathcal{C}}\) create new \(\partial \rho_{\mathcal{C}} / \partial n = 0\) points, i.e., temporary acupoints.

§4 Geometric Correspondence with “Proportional Bone Measurement”

Traditional Chinese medicine uses “Proportional Bone Measurement” (Tong Shen Cun — the patient’s own thumb width, four-finger width, etc.) as an individualized metric for acupoint localization. This article demonstrates that the essence of Proportional Bone Measurement is the individual normalization of the 𝒞-sector standing wave scale.

Theorem 10.7.4.01 (Geometric Correspondence of Proportional Bone Measurement). Let the Proportional Bone Measurement of individual \(i\) be \(L_i\), and the standard Proportional Bone Measurement be \(L_0\). Then:

\[\frac{L_i}{L_0} = \frac{\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}^{(i)}}{\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}^0} = \frac{\theta_\tau^0}{\theta_\tau^{(i)}}\]

where \(\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}^{(i)}\) is the 𝒞-sector standing wave wavelength of individual \(i\).

Proof. By 10.3 §1.1, \(\lambda_{\mathcal{C}} = 2L/m\), where \(L\) is the limb segment length. Since \(L\) itself is jointly modulated by \(\sigma_M\) and \(\theta_\tau\), the ratio \(\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}^{(i)} / \lambda_{\mathcal{C}}^0\) is proportional to the Proportional Bone Measurement ratio \(L_i/L_0\). The proportionality coefficient is determined by the individual variation in \(\theta_\tau\). \(\square\)
